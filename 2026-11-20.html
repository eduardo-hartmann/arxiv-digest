1. Eigenspectra of Poisson-sampled stellar population models: Statistical fingerprints of cool giant stars in unresolved early-type galaxies

Anastasia Gvozdenko, Russell J. Smith · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF · 💻 code/data · 💻 code/data

Why it was picked: PCA method on stellar population models to extract cool-giant signatures from unresolved ETG spectra — squarely core stellar population synthesis/ML methodology. — score 8.0 (you 9/10 · field 5/10)

TL;DR. The authors extend a PCA-based "eigenspectra" method for extracting statistical fluctuation signals from Poisson-sampled stellar population models, showing that across 0.45–2.4 \(\mu\)m, ages 3–13 Gyr, and metallicities \(-0.3\) to \(+0.3\) dex, pixel-to-pixel spectral variance in unresolved early-type galaxy (ETG) light is overwhelmingly dominated by one component tracing the coolest, most luminous giant stars [p. 1].

Main question

Can one isolate the spectral contribution of specific stellar types — especially cool giants — from unresolved (integrated-light) spectra of old galaxies, given that standard integrated-light fitting mixes all stellar types together and is known to be highly sensitive to uncertain treatment of cool giants (e.g., a 2–5% shift in cool-dwarf light fraction can mimic a Salpeter-vs-Kroupa IMF change) [§1, p. 1]? This matters because cool giants are among the most poorly constrained ingredients in population synthesis, with documented mismatches such as underpredicted near-IR CO bands in massive ellipticals and possible exotic stellar populations (helium-enhanced stars, peculiar CO-strong stars in M87) that current isochrones may not capture [§1, pp. 1–2]. The paper builds on Smith (2022, "S22"), who introduced PCA on Poisson-sampled model spectra in the I-band for one age/metallicity, and asks whether this signal generalizes across wavelength, age, and metallicity [§1, p. 2].

Data & method

The authors generate synthetic "IFU pixel" spectra by Poisson-sampling PARSEC v1.2S isochrones (with the COLIBRI AGB extension) [§2.1, p. 3], assigning empirical stellar spectra via the spigen interpolator (MILES + IRTF libraries, 0.35–2.5 \(\mu\)m) [§2.1, p. 3], and summing stars drawn from a Salpeter IMF (\(\alpha=2.35\)) scaled to a fixed total luminosity of \(10\,000\,L_\odot\) per realization (comparable to a 0.05–0.1 arcsec pixel at the effective radius of a 10 Mpc elliptical) [§2.1, p. 3]. They explore ages 3, 6, 10, 13 Gyr and [Fe/H] \(=-0.3,0.0,+0.3\), restricting to ages \(>3\) Gyr to avoid uncertain thermally-pulsing AGB/carbon-star physics [§2.1, p. 3]. 1000 realizations per model are generated, divided into five wavelength windows (V, I, J, H, K, spanning roughly 0.45–2.4 \(\mu\)m) [§2.1, §3, pp. 3, 5], continuum-normalized via a low-order polynomial fit, then decomposed with standard PCA into eigenspectra \(E_i\) and eigenvalues \(P_i\) [§2.2, p. 4]. They also test simple bimodal mixtures of two ages or two metallicities to approximate composite populations [§4.2, p. 9], and compare PCA eigenspectra to two alternative fluctuation techniques: the per-channel SBF spectrum (Mitzkus et al. 2018) and the fluctuation-ratio spectrum (van Dokkum & Conroy 2014) [§2.3, p. 4].

Main results

  1. In nearly every case, two PCs capture the dominant variance (>95% in all bands, Table 1 [p. 7]; Fig. 1 [p. 5] shows 99% for the I-band reference case). The first eigenspectrum generically takes the form of a late-M giant spectrum, dominated by TiO bands, representing the most massive/coolest giants present; the second relates to "evenness" of sampling along the giant branch [§2.3, Fig. 2, p. 6].
  2. Wavelength matters: in the IR (H, K bands) the integrated light is already giant-dominated, so \(E_1\) resembles the mean spectrum but still suppresses warmer-star features and enhances CO/\({}^{13}\)CO bandheads [Figs. 5–6, pp. 8–9]. In the optical (V band), by contrast, the brightest contributing stars are intermediate-temperature giants (~3700 K) rather than the coolest tip-RGB stars, and \(E_1\) reveals TiO bands from ~M2-type (~3500 K) giants that are nearly invisible in the integrated light — qualitatively different behavior from the SBF method, which instead tracks the brightest star at each wavelength independently and drifts warmer toward the blue [Figs. 7–8, p. 10–11, §3 discussion p. 7].
  3. Results are robust to age (3–13 Gyr) but show modest, systematic dependence on metallicity, reflecting changes in giant-branch temperature; e.g., in the I-band, higher metallicity strengthens redder TiO bandheads and weakens bluer ones monotonically [Figs. 9–10, p. 9]. Composite bimodal-age or bimodal-metallicity mixtures produce only modest extra effects, with metallicity mixtures tending to track the metal-poor or near-solar component depending on band, largest in J and H bands [§4.2, Figs. 12–16, pp. 9–12].
  4. Table 1 [p. 7] quantifies the effective number of stars \(\bar N\) driving fluctuations per band, dropping from \(\bar N\sim360\) (V) to \(\bar N\sim3.6\) (K) at fixed total luminosity, confirming that redder bands are dominated by a handful of extreme giants.

Caveats & limitations

The authors note the method is insensitive to age and (largely) to the IMF, so it cannot constrain these parameters — its value is instead in testing whether model giant spectra match real populations [§5, p. 12]. They restrict to ages \(>3\) Gyr to avoid uncertain TP-AGB/carbon-star physics, explicitly excluding younger populations from this paper (though mentioned as future/companion work: Smith & Gvozdenko 2026) [§2.1, p. 3; §5, p. 13]. Stellar-evolution stage (RGB vs AGB) is not distinguished in spectral assignment, which the authors flag as a modeling limitation relevant to interpreting \({}^{13}\)CO enhancement [footnote, p. 7]. Observationally, the method requires many independent, spatially uniform IFU samples at high angular resolution, which in practice restricts targets/facilities (HARMONI, MAVIS, JWST suggested) [§5, p. 13]. My own observation: all results here are purely theoretical/model-based (no real observational validation is attempted in this paper itself, though referenced in a companion paper), and the models use just one IMF slope and no abundance-ratio variations, so sensitivity to those dimensions (highlighted as important for ETGs in the Introduction) is not tested here.

Assessment

This is a methodological/model paper rather than an observational result — it validates and generalizes an existing idea (S22) rather than introducing a fundamentally new technique, but the systematic mapping across wavelength, age, and metallicity is a genuinely useful groundwork contribution for anyone wanting to apply PCA-fluctuation spectroscopy to real IFU data. For a stellar-population/full-spectrum-fitting audience, the key takeaway is that this is a complementary, largely model-independent diagnostic for the cool-giant uncertainties that plague IMF and abundance inference in massive ETGs (the CO-band and bottom-heavy-IMF puzzles mentioned in §1 and §5) — not a replacement for integrated-light fitting, but a cross-check. It connects to spectroscopic survey/ML interests only tangentially (this is a classical PCA/eigenspectrum decomposition on simulated spectra, not a deep-learning or large-survey application), but the general idea of using sample-to-sample stochastic variance as an information channel is conceptually relevant to anyone working on uncertainty/representation learning in spectra.

Code & data

Data products (PCA-ready stellar population spectra) are available at https://github.com/agvozdenko/stellar-population-pca; the authors note that due to stochastic (Poisson) realizations, released files will not exactly reproduce numbers in the paper, though qualitative conclusions hold [Data Availability, pp. 13–14].

2. Influence of variable IMF on the dynamical black hole mass estimates in massive ETGs

Avinash Chaturvedi, Sabine Thater, Davor Krajnovi\'{c}, Ignacio Mart\'{i}n-Navarro, Alina Boecker · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF

Why it was picked: Variable IMF from IFU stellar-population modeling affecting dynamical BH masses, core topic and co-authored by followed researcher Martín-Navarro. — score 7.0 (you 8/10 · field 4/10)

TL;DR. The authors remeasure black hole masses in five massive early-type galaxies using Schwarzschild dynamical modelling, but this time let the stellar mass-to-light ratio vary radially according to a spatially resolved, free-IMF stellar-population fit instead of assuming it is constant — finding galaxy-dependent BH mass shifts from -83% to +60% relative to constant-M*/L models [p. 1].

Main question

The paper asks how much dynamical black hole (BH) mass estimates change when a radially varying stellar initial mass function (IMF) — and the resulting variable stellar mass-to-light ratio (M/L) — is incorporated into orbit-based dynamical mass models, instead of the usual assumption of a constant M/L [p. 1]. This matters because stellar-population studies increasingly find bottom-heavy (bio Salpeter-like or steeper), radially declining IMFs in massive ETG centres [p. 1-2], and since BH mass, stellar mass, and dark matter are degenerate components of the same gravitational potential, an incorrect stellar M*/L profile could bias BH masses used to calibrate the \(M_{BH}-\sigma\) and related scaling relations [§1, p. 2].

Data & method

The sample is five massive ETGs (NGC3923, NGC4261, NGC4636, IC4296, IC4329) drawn from the "SMASHING" sample of Thater et al. 2026 (Thater2026), all with SINFONI and MUSE IFU data [§2, p. 2]. Kinematics come from pPXF full-spectrum fitting on Voronoi-binned spectra (target S/N 50-70 for SINFONI; S/N 50, or 150 for NGC4636, for MUSE) [§2.1, p. 2-3]. Stellar population/IMF maps are derived with the "full index fitting" (FIF) method of Martín-Navarro et al., which fits five gravity- and IMF-sensitive absorption features (Fe5270, Fe5335, Mgb5177, TiO1, TiO2) using MILES α-variable SSP models with a bimodal IMF parametrized by high-mass-end slope \(\Gamma_b\) (Kroupa-like \(\Gamma_b=1.3\), Salpeter-like \(\Gamma_b=1.35\)) [§3, p. 3]. These M*/L maps are mapped onto Multi-Gaussian Expansion (MGE) surface-brightness models (from Thater2026) and fed into triaxial Schwarzschild orbit modelling with DYNAMITE v4.0, which also includes an NFW dark-matter halo and a central point-mass BH, fit in two steps (shape/DM first, then BH mass and a stellar-mass scaling factor \(S\) on a refined grid) [§4, p. 3-8].

Main results

  1. All five galaxies show bottom-heavy IMFs in their centres that decline (become lighter) with radius, shown in the 2D maps of Fig. 4 [p. 6] and 1D radial profiles of Fig. 5 [p. 8]; NGC4261 stays bottom-heavy even at large radii, while NGC4636, IC4296 and IC4329 decline to Kroupa/Milky-Way-like values outward [p. 6-7].
  2. The IMF-metallicity and IMF-\(\sigma\) relations are galaxy-dependent, shown in Fig. 9 [p. 12]: NGC4636, IC4296, IC4329 correlate strongly with metallicity (Spearman \(\rho=1.00, 0.855, 0.927\)), while NGC3923 and NGC4261 correlate instead with velocity dispersion (\(\rho=1.00, 0.952\)) — no single parameter universally tracks IMF slope across the sample [§6.1, p. 12].
  3. With the variable-M*/L models, Schwarzschild fits (Fig. 7, p. 9; Table 2, p. 10) give constrained BH masses for NGC3923 (\(1.99^{+6.76}_{-1.05}\times10^8\,M_\odot\)) and IC4329 (\(21.78^{+13.58}_{-11.32}\times10^8\,M_\odot\)), and 3σ upper limits for NGC4261 (\(<28.27\times10^8\,M_\odot\)), NGC4636 (\(<12.40\times10^8\,M_\odot\)), and IC4296 (\(<33.57\times10^8\,M_\odot\)) [Tab. 2, p. 10].
  4. Compared to Thater2026's constant-M*/L models, the main result (Fig. 10, p. 14, and §6.3, p. 13) is that the effect is strongly galaxy-dependent and non-systematic: BH mass drops by ~83% for NGC3923 and ~11% for IC4329, while upper limits rise by ~27% (NGC4261) and ~60% (NGC4636) but fall by ~51% (IC4296) [§6.3, p. 13].

Caveats & limitations

The authors note: SINFONI data quality is limited (velocity-dispersion errors ~10%) for NGC4261, NGC4636 and IC4296, so these galaxies yield only upper limits, not detections, and complex kinematic structures reduce sensitivity to \(M_{BH}\) [§5.2.2, p. 11]; the TiO2 feature (and thus IMF sensitivity) had to be excluded for NGC3923 and IC4329 due to skyline contamination, so NGC3923's steep outer IMF decline "should be interpreted with caution" [§3, p. 3; §5.1, p. 6-7]; the dark-matter halo is only weakly constrained (large 3σ ranges) even though stellar mass is better constrained [§5.2.1, p. 9]; because the MGE stellar models extend beyond the MUSE field of view, M*/L is extrapolated as a constant beyond ~15-20 arcsec, a potential source of systematic error [§4.1, p. 4]; and the sample is only five galaxies, explicitly flagged as too small to establish a universal IMF driver and motivating future larger samples [§6.1, p. 13; §7, p. 15]. My own observation: the paper does not discuss how sensitive the BH-mass shifts are to the choice of IMF functional form (bimodal only) or to the FIF method's known model-dependence, nor does it propagate IMF-fit uncertainty into the final \(M_{BH}\) uncertainties shown in Table 2.

Assessment

This is a careful, methodologically solid follow-up to Thater2026 and to Thater et al. (2023)'s FCC47 result, and it is useful precisely because the result is not a clean "BH masses are systematically overestimated by X%" story — the galaxy-to-galaxy variation (from -83% to +60%) is itself the finding, and it's a sobering one for anyone citing \(M_{BH}-\sigma\) or \(M_{BH}-M_*\) relations built from constant-M*/L dynamical masses. For this researcher's interests, the most relevant piece is less the BH-mass number itself and more the FIF-based radial IMF/stellar-population maps (Fig. 4, Fig. 9) that directly connect spatially resolved spectroscopy to physical stellar-population inference, and the explicit demonstration that no single parameter (metallicity vs. \(\sigma\)) universally explains IMF variation — a reminder that single-galaxy or small-sample IMF-driver claims may not generalize. The sample size (5 galaxies) limits how much can be concluded about the BH mass systematics in general; this reads as a proof-of-concept for a method the authors themselves say needs scaling up.

Code & data

DYNAMITE (v4.0) is described as "publicly available" with a citation to Jethwa et al. 2020 and Thater et al. 2022a [§4.2, p. 4]; MGE fitting via the mgefit package is linked at https://pypi.org/project/mgefit/ [p. 3, footnote]. No dedicated code/data repository for this paper's analysis is given.

3. On the feasibility of using [Eu/\(\alpha\)] to identify accreted and in-situ extragalactic globular clusters

Anastasia Gvozdenko, Michael A. Beasley, Katja Fahrion, S{\o}ren Larsen, Francesco La Barbera, Alexandre Vazdekis et al. · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF · 💻 code/data

Why it was picked: Integrated-light abundance diagnostics ([Eu/alpha]) for extragalactic globular clusters, core stellar-population chemistry topic, co-authored by followed researcher Martín-Navarro. — score 7.0 (you 8/10 · field 4/10)

TL;DR. Using a homogeneous sample of 28 Local Group globular clusters (21 extragalactic, 7 Milky Way) with integrated-light Eu and Si abundances, the authors show that low [Eu/Si] (~−0.4) marks in-situ GCs and high [Eu/Si] (~+0.4) marks accreted GCs across M31, M33 and several dwarfs, extending a Milky Way-calibrated diagnostic to other galaxies for the first time [Abstract, p. 1].

Main question

The paper asks whether the abundance ratio [Eu/α] (specifically [Eu/Si]), already used to separate accreted from in-situ stellar populations and globular clusters (GCs) in the Milky Way, also works as an accretion tracer in other Local Group galaxies [§1, p. 1-2]. This matters because GCs are long-lived, bright tracers of galaxy assembly, and a chemical tag that survives beyond the Milky Way would let researchers reconstruct accretion histories of external galaxies from integrated-light spectroscopy alone, without needing resolved stellar kinematics [§1, p. 1].

Data & method

The core dataset is high-resolution, integrated-light abundance measurements for 46 Local Group GCs from Larsen et al. (2022, Keck/HIRES and VLT/UVES spectra) plus one additional GC in Sextans A from Gvozdenko et al. (2024), analysed with a consistent SSP-fitting pipeline (MARCS/TURBOSPECTRUM and ATLAS12/SYNTHE atmospheres, NLTE corrections for several elements) [§2, p. 2]. Of these, 28 GCs (21 extragalactic + 7 Milky Way) have both Si and Eu measured, forming the primary sample, listed in Table 1 [p. 4]. The authors also fold in M31 bulge abundances from La Barbera et al. (2026), derived from OSIRIS/GTC long-slit spectra fit with E-MILES/Conroy et al. (2018) models, including a newly derived stacked-spectrum measurement (200–600 pc) for elements (Cr, Mn, Ba, Ni, Cu, Eu) not in the original La Barbera analysis [§2, Appendix A, p. 2, 11]. GCs are classified in/ex-situ by comparing their [Eu/Si]–[Fe/H] position to the empirical in-situ/accreted trend lines defined by Monty et al. (2024) from Milky Way data; a cluster is "ambiguous" if its error bar crosses both trends [§3.2, p. 3].

Main results

  1. Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 [p. 5-6] show the central result: metal-rich M31 GCs ([Fe/H] > −0.7) sit at [Eu/Si] ≈ −0.4, consistent with in-situ formation, while lower-metallicity M31 GCs scatter to [Eu/Si] ≈ +0.4, consistent with accretion — mirroring the Milky Way trend and extending it to a second massive galaxy [§4.2, p. 5-6]. Discriminating power is best at intermediate metallicity (−2 < [Fe/H] < −1) and is lost at the metal-poor end where the two trend lines converge [§3.2, §4.5, p. 3, 9].
  2. Table 1 [p. 4] gives the full in-situ/accreted classification for all 28 GCs; for the 7 MW GCs these classifications broadly match independent kinematic/age-based literature classifications (Massari 2025), though two clusters (NGC 7078, NGC 7099) are classified differently than in Monty et al. (2024), illustrating low-metallicity ambiguity [§4.1, p. 3].
  3. Fig. 4 [p. 7] shows the projected spatial distribution of M31 GCs on PAndAS photometry: the chemically in-situ GCs sit at smaller projected radii while chemically accreted (outer-halo) GCs sit farther out, though the authors stress radius alone cannot establish origin [§4.2, p. 5-7].
  4. Fig. 5 [p. 8] compares 11 elements (Na, Mg, Si, Ti, Ca, Eu, Cr, Mn, Ba, Ni, Cu) between the metal-rich M31 GCs and the M31 bulge: most elements agree well, except Ba (and possibly Cr, Ti), and [Eu/Si] is lower in the GCs than the bulge — interpreted as GCs having formed on shorter timescales than the bulk of the bulge [§4.2, p. 6-7]. For M33 and intermediate-mass dwarfs the authors find a mix of in-situ and accreted GCs, whereas lower-mass dwarfs (WLM, Sextans A, Fornax) are dominated by in-situ classifications, leading to the inference that accreted GCs in massive hosts came, at least partly, from intermediate-mass, intermediate-metallicity progenitors [§4.4-4.5, Conclusions, p. 7-10].

Caveats & limitations

The authors explicitly note: the Monty et al. (2024) trend lines are empirical fits to Milky Way data, not derived from chemical-evolution models, so applying them to M31 or other galaxies assumes similar enrichment pathways, which "should be treated with caution" given M31's likely different assembly history [§4.5, p. 9]. The [Eu/Si] diagnostic loses discriminating power at [Fe/H] < −1.6-2.0, where in-situ/accreted tracks converge, so accreted GCs at the lowest metallicities could be missed [§3.2, §4.5, p. 3, 9]. Several individual classifications conflict with other diagnostics — e.g. NGC 6822's SC7 is classified in-situ via [Eu/Si] but accreted-like via luminosity, ellipticity and low [α/Fe] in other work [§4.4, p. 8-9]; M31 chemical classifications also don't always match substructure-based classifications from proper-motion/photometric studies [§4.2, p. 6]. Sample sizes per galaxy are small (e.g. only 1-6 GCs per dwarf, 3 for M33), and some [Eu/Si] values rely on converted [Eu/Ca] measurements from a different study (Sakari et al. 2015) via a calibrated relation with 0.06 dex scatter [Appendix C, p. 13]. My own observation: the paper doesn't discuss how measurement systematics between the Larsen/Gvozdenko integrated-light analysis and the La Barbera bulge analysis (different fitting methods, LTE vs NLTE treatment) might bias the GC-bulge comparison beyond the small offsets they attribute to methodology [§4.2, p. 6].

Assessment

This is a focused, well-controlled abundance study rather than a methods or survey paper — its value lies in demonstrating, with a genuinely homogeneous integrated-light dataset, that a Galactic chemical-tagging diagnostic transfers to external galaxies, which is a non-trivial and useful validation for extragalactic archaeology. The sample sizes are small by survey standards (tens of GCs, single digits per galaxy), so the statistical conclusions (e.g., "intermediate-mass progenitors build massive-galaxy accreted populations") are suggestive rather than conclusive, and the authors are appropriately cautious about this. For someone working on stellar populations and spectroscopic surveys, the main transferable insight is methodological: full-spectrum fitting of integrated-light spectra can recover detailed abundances (including r-process elements) for unresolved stellar systems, which is directly relevant to what full-spectrum fitting pipelines can extract from galaxy/GC spectra in surveys like DESI or WEAVE, even though this paper itself uses targeted high-resolution spectroscopy rather than a large survey. It is a nice complement to the galaxy-wide stellar-population work (e.g. the companion M31 bulge analysis by La Barbera et al. 2026) since it shows GC and bulge abundance patterns can be cross-compared with the same framework.

Code & data

The integrated-light fitting code iSpy3 is referenced via its GitHub repository [footnote, p. 2]. The paper states the underlying data are available in the cited literature (Larsen et al. 2022; Gvozdenko et al. 2024; La Barbera et al. 2026), with additional M31 bulge measurements provided in the article and appendix, and further data products available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request [Data Availability, p. 10].

4. Off-Nuclear AGN Signatures in MaNGA: Spatially resolved selection using MEGACUBES

Sandra Jaison, Rog\'erio Riffel, Dominika Wylezalek, Sandro B. Rembold, Sandra I. Raimundo, Rogemar A. Riffel et al. · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF

Why it was picked: Large MaNGA IFU spectroscopic sample with emission-line diagnostics, matches core interest in large-survey galaxy spectroscopy catalogs. — score 6.5 (you 7/10 · field 5/10)

TL;DR. Using "megacube" spatially-resolved spectral fits for the full MaNGA DR17 sample (~10,000 galaxies), the authors build a multi-diagnostic (BPT+WHaN+WHaD) spatial pipeline that finds 261 nuclear, 201 off-nuclear, and 159 "quiet" AGN candidates, and show that off-nuclear AGN hosts are statistically identical to nuclear AGN hosts in mass/age/SFR/metallicity, implying the nuclear/off-nuclear distinction is a property of the ionized gas, not of the galaxy [Abstract, p. 1].

Main question

The paper asks whether AGN-like ionization found away from a galaxy's photometric center ("off-nuclear AGN") in integral-field spectroscopy marks a physically distinct class of host galaxy (e.g., a displaced/wandering SMBH) or is instead a local, gas-level phenomenon (projection, dilution, or a recently faded nucleus) [§1, p. 2–3]. This matters because single-fiber surveys like SDSS only sample galaxy centers and are known to miss obscured, low-luminosity, or spatially offset AGN, biasing any census of SMBH activity and its role in quenching/feedback [§1, p. 2].

Data & method

The sample is the complete MaNGA DR17 survey (>10,000 nearby galaxies, \(z\sim0.03\) on average, \(3600\)–\(10400\) Å, \(R\sim2000\)) [§2.1, p. 2]. Physical and emission-line maps come from "megacubes" (Riffel et al. 2023), produced with the urutau pipeline doing spaxel-by-spaxel full spectral fitting with starlight (GRANADA+MILES models plus a power-law AGN continuum) and ifscube Gaussian emission-line fits [§2.2, p. 3]. AGN spaxels are classified with [N II]- and [S II]-BPT diagrams, the WHaN diagram (EW(H\(\alpha\)) vs. [N II]/H\(\alpha\)), and the WHaD diagram (EW(H\(\alpha\)) vs. \(\sigma_{\rm H\alpha}\)) [§3.1, p. 3–4]. A refined version of the Wylezalek et al. (2018) spaxel-fraction technique — with EW(H\(\alpha\))>3 Å, surface-brightness, and BPT-distance (\(d_{\rm BPT}\)) cuts [§3.2, p. 4] — selects 776 global AGN candidates, visually vetted (removing Type-1/broad-line and major-merger systems) down to 627 galaxies [Tab. 1, p. 7]. These are then split spatially: "nuclear" if ≥40% of spaxels in a 2 kpc aperture are active in BPT+WHaN jointly; otherwise tested in four cumulative radial zones (1–2, 1–4, 1–6 kpc, and out to the field edge) for an off-nuclear fraction >15% [§4.1, p. 5–6]; sources failing both are "quiet" [§4.2, p. 6–7].

Main results

  1. The final census is 261 nuclear, 201 off-nuclear, and 159 quiet AGN [Tab. 1, p. 7], cross-validated against Wylezalek et al. (2018) (105 in common, differences traced to DRP pipeline changes between MPL-5 and DR17; Fig. 3, p. 8), Albán & Wylezalek (2023) (190 in common), and Riffel et al. (2023) single-fiber AGN (171 in common) [§5.1, p. 7–8].
  2. Nuclear and off-nuclear AGN hosts are statistically indistinguishable in stellar mass, age, SFR, stellar kinematics, and gas-phase metallicity (e.g., \(\langle12+\log({\rm O/H})\rangle\sim8.63\)–\(8.76\) for both) [Tab. 2, p. 9; Fig. 7, p. 9], but the AGN-ionized gas itself differs sharply: nuclear spaxels are brighter in [O III]\(\lambda\)5007 by \(\sim\)0.7 dex, have higher excitation (log([O III]/H\(\beta\))=0.50 vs. 0.29), and broader \(\sigma_{\rm gas}\) (138 vs. 120 km/s) [Tab. 2, Fig. 5–6, p. 9].
  3. Orientation matters but doesn't fully explain the effect: 72% of nuclear hosts are face-on vs. 50% of off-nuclear hosts [Fig. 8, p. 10], yet the [O III] luminosity and excitation deficits persist in all three inclination bins (Table 3, p. 11; Fig. 9, p. 11), and nuclear-region Balmer decrements show comparable dust columns in edge-on nuclear vs. off-nuclear hosts (§5.3.2, p. 10) — arguing against simple dust-obscuration as the sole driver.
  4. The classical WHaN SF/AGN boundary at log([N II]/H\(\alpha\))=−0.4 misclassifies high-metallicity diffuse ionized gas as AGN in spatially resolved data; shifting it to −0.25 (Eq. 1, p. 7) removes 48,735 spurious AGN spaxels from the "quiet" sample, 94% of them outside the 2 kpc nucleus [§5.4, p. 10–11]. Combining this with fading-AGN/fossil-outflow comparisons (Casey et al. 2026; T. Brosi et al., submitted) and a Chandra X-ray cross-match (§6.5–6.6, p. 13–14), they estimate an episodic AGN phase lifetime of \(t_{\rm AGN}=(4.47\pm0.15)\times10^4\) yr, consistent within a factor of a few with independent fading-AGN timescales [§6.5, p. 14].

Caveats & limitations

The authors note: their off-nuclear sample is a strict lower limit since any true offset <1 kpc blends into the nuclear classification [§4.1.2, p. 6]; results depend on the MaNGA DRP version (pipeline updates between MPL-5 and DR17 moved spaxels across the AGN/non-AGN boundary) [§5.1.1, p. 7]; the choice of BPT diagnostic matters substantially for off-nuclear (but not nuclear) classifications — a 3D framework (Ji & Yan 2020) yields only 99 off-nuclear AGN vs. 201 with the standard 2D scheme, and only 38/201 survive all three schemes tested [§5.5, Tab. 5, p. 12]; the DIG correction (−0.25 shift) is empirical, not physically derived; and reconciling SF vs. AGN metallicity calibrations onto one absolute scale remains unresolved [§6.4, p. 13]. Edge-on-bin statistics are explicitly flagged as needing confirmation with larger samples [§6.4, p. 13]. My own observations: beam smearing at \(z\gtrsim0.06\) (where the 2 kpc aperture approaches the MaNGA resolution element) could bias nuclear fractions for distant galaxies, which the authors mention only briefly [§4.1.1, p. 6]; and the Chandra/radio validation subsamples are very small (6 and tens of objects), so the physical-mechanism conclusions in §6.1 and §6.6 rest on limited multiwavelength statistics.

Assessment

TL;DR. Main question

The paper asks how much useful per-source information a compact self-supervised model can retain from heterogeneous Euclid catalogue photometry alone, without requiring uniform measurement coverage across sources [§1, p. 1-2]. This matters because Euclid's MER catalogues mix Euclid and ground-based photometry (DECam, HSC, Pan-STARRS, MegaCam) with varying depth and coverage across fields, so requiring complete measurements in fixed channels discards information or excludes sources [§1, p. 1]. The authors frame this as a step toward catalogue-level foundation models, complementary to existing image-based (AstroPT) and image–spectrum contrastive (AstroCLIP) approaches [§1, p. 1-2].

Data & method

The working sample is 90,258 Euclid Quick Data Release 1 (Q1) sources drawn from an initial random 100,000-object sample split roughly equally across EDF-North, EDF-South, and EDF-Fornax, after quality filtering [§2.1, p. 2]. Each source has 29–70 available flux/uncertainty measurements (median 64) out of 94 defined channels (survey × band × measurement type) [§2.1, p. 2]. Fluxes and uncertainties are inverse-hyperbolic-sine transformed and combined with categorical (survey/band/type) and positional embeddings [§2.1, p. 2]. The model is an eight-layer bidirectional Transformer encoder (BERT-like, not autoregressive like AstroPT) with a four-layer decoder; during pretraining 20 available channels per source are randomly withheld and reconstructed, with loss computed only on masked, available positions [§2.2, Fig. 2, p. 2-3; Eq. 1, A6, p. 3,11]. An 80:20 train/validation split (72,206/18,052 sources) is used, trained with AdamW for up to 1,000 epochs, best checkpoint at epoch 656 [App. A.4, p. 11]. The frozen [CLS] token gives a 128-dim embedding per source [§2.2, p. 3]. A "Platinum Sample" of 9,675 labelled sources (2,675 spectroscopic quasars, 3,500 Gaia-validated stars, 3,500 DES/VHS-validated galaxies) trains a small MLP classifier (two hidden layers, 64/32 units) on the frozen embeddings [§2.3-2.4, p. 3-4].

Main results

  1. Classification on the curated test set is strong: the MLP reaches 97.3% overall accuracy, with per-class precision/recall/F1 around 0.95-0.99 (Tab. 1, p. 4).
  2. Morphology and brightness are encoded: Fig. 3 [p. 5] shows high point-like-probability sources cluster in embedding space (12.4 of 15 nearest neighbours high-probability on average, vs. 4.3 under a field/magnitude-matched random baseline) [§3.1, p. 4]. Linear models trained on embeddings from two fields predict VIS magnitude in the third with Spearman correlations of 0.954–0.980 (§3.2, p. 5), and nearby sources in CLS space agree in VIS magnitude in 76.4% of pair comparisons vs. ~50% baseline [§3.2, p. 5].
  3. Uncertainty information is present but weak locally: for the 2,026 sources with σI > 1 µJy, adding CLS coordinates to a magnitude-only model reduces RMSE in predicted log uncertainty by 24-37% across held-out fields, though local neighbourhood agreement in reported uncertainty is close to random (Fig. 5, §3.3, p. 6-7).
  4. Catalogue-level quasar classification does not transfer well: applying calibrated thresholds to the full catalogue yields 1,150 quasar candidates, but of the 156 candidates with accepted external counterparts, only 21 show AGN/QSO evidence (12 spectroscopic), versus 359/361 correct for the 462 stellar candidates matched to Gaia (Tab. 2, Fig. 6, §3.5-3.6, p. 7-9). The authors explicitly state the classifier's performance on the Platinum (held-out) test set does not generalize to the broader working catalogue, likely because the highly selective Platinum sample is not representative of the full photometric catalogue [§3.6, §4, p. 8].

Caveats & limitations

The authors state: external counterparts exist for fewer than 1% of the 90,258 sources, so most candidates are unverified [§4, p. 8]; the Platinum sample is class-balanced and brighter than the catalogue, so quoted precision/recall apply only to that validation sample [§2.4, p. 4]; quasar purity/completeness "remain to be established" [§3.6, p. 8]; the "94 channels" are not independent observables since several share underlying images [§2.1, A.2, p. 2,10]; VIS-band uncertainty tests exclude the inflated-uncertainty tail (σI > 1 µJy) as a sensitivity check rather than modeling it directly [§3.3, p. 6]; the field-structure analysis (§3.4, p. 7) is only descriptive, with causal attribution to shared survey coverage left untested. My own observations: the quasar failure mode (21/156) suggests substantial selection-function mismatch between Golden/Platinum spectroscopic samples and the photometric population that is not fully diagnosed; the paper also does not compare embeddings against a simple baseline classifier trained directly on raw photometry, so it's unclear how much the self-supervised pretraining itself helps versus the MLP head alone.

Assessment

This is a methodologically careful but modest-scale proof-of-concept (90k sources, demo-sized model per Fig. 1, p. 2) rather than a mature foundation model — the authors themselves frame it as "a step toward" catalogue-level foundation models [§1, p. 2] and are unusually candid that the quasar classifier likely does not transfer [§3.6, p. 8], which is refreshing and increases my trust in the other claims. For this researcher's interests, the most relevant angle is methodological: it's a masked-autoencoder/BERT-style analogue to AstroPT/AstroCLIP applied purely to catalogue photometry (not spectra or images) [§1, p. 1-2], illustrating how embedding-based representations could complement full-spectrum fitting approaches to stellar/galaxy classification, though it does not touch stellar population parameters (age, metallicity, SFH) at all. The quasar-contamination result is a useful cautionary case study for anyone building classifiers from small spectroscopically-selected training sets and applying them to much larger photometric populations — a generalization-gap problem directly relevant to ML-for-surveys work.
