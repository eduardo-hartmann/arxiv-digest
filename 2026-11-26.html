1. Tipping the scales in Blue Jay: Dust energy balance overpredicts IR luminosity in galaxies at \(z\sim2\)

Benjamin P. Collins, Sirio Belli, Letizia Bugiani, Amir H. Khoram, Minjung Park, Vanessa M. Brown et al. · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF · 💻 code/data

Why it was picked: Tests a core SED-fitting assumption (dust energy balance) using JWST photometry/spectroscopy of z~2 galaxies, directly relevant to physical property inference methodology. — score 7.8 (you 8/10 · field 7/10)

TL;DR. Testing dust energy balance (the assumption that UV/optical-absorbed starlight equals re-emitted IR) against 89 star-forming cosmic-noon galaxies with new JWST/MIRI photometry, the authors find SED models fit to HST+NIRCam alone overpredict mid-IR fluxes by ~10% at 7.7µm up to a factor of ~3 at 21µm, with the overprediction growing strongly with dust attenuation [Abstract, p. 1].

Main question

The paper asks whether "dust energy balance" — the assumption used by many SED-fitting codes (Cigale, Magphys, Prospector) that energy absorbed by dust in the UV-optical equals energy re-emitted in the IR — actually holds for galaxies at cosmic noon (\(z\sim2\)) [§1, pp. 1-2]. This matters because LIR estimated this way feeds directly into inferred star-formation histories and dust content [p. 2], and because measuring attenuation along one line of sight may not represent a galaxy's global, isotropic dust emission, especially in asymmetric or inclined systems [p. 2]. Direct IR data to test this have been rare at high z until JWST/MIRI.

Data & method

The sample is drawn from the Blue Jay survey (153 galaxies, NIRSpec MSA spectroscopy, \(1.7<z<3.5\), COSMOS field) [§2.1, p. 2]. The authors perform new aperture photometry on archival JWST/MIRI imaging (PRIMER, COSMOS-Web, COSMOS-3D) for 111 Blue Jay galaxies, using a custom pipeline ("miriutils") with astrometric correction, local background modelling, and PSF-based aperture corrections [§3, pp. 3-5]. Photometry is validated and calibrated (×1.182) against the COSMOS2025 catalogue [§3.3, p. 5, Fig. 3 p. 5]. After cuts (missing redshifts, blending, AGN, high-z fillers), the working sample is 89 star-forming galaxies [§4, p. 6]. SEDs are fit with Prospector (FSPS-based, Chabrier IMF, non-parametric SFH, Charlot & Fall 2000 dust attenuation, Draine & Li 2007 dust emission under energy balance) using only HST+NIRCam photometry; predicted MIRI fluxes are then computed by convolving posterior model spectra with MIRI filter curves and compared to the real measured MIRI fluxes [§4.1, pp. 6-7]. They repeat fits adding NIRSpec spectroscopy, and separately adding the real MIRI photometry itself, to see how predictions change [§4.4-4.6, pp. 9-11].

Main results

  1. Mid-IR fluxes are systematically overpredicted, worsening with wavelength: median offset rises from 0.04 dex (10%) in F770W to 0.47 dex (factor ~3) in F2100W, shown via flux comparison (Fig. 6, p. 9) and normalized-residual distributions (Fig. 7, p. 9).
  2. The overprediction tracks dust attenuation, not SFR: in Fig. 8 [p. 9] and Fig. 9 [p. 10], galaxies with \(A_V\gtrsim1.5\) show the mean log flux ratio climbing to +0.63 dex (~factor 4.4 overprediction) in F2100W, while low-attenuation galaxies (\(A_V\lesssim1\)) show no significant offset; sSFR100 plays only a weak secondary role.
  3. Adding NIRSpec spectroscopy (which breaks dust-age-metallicity degeneracies but contains no IR information) cuts the F2100W overprediction from ~factor 3 (+0.39 dex) to ~factor 1.5 (+0.18 dex), with scatter dropping from 0.56 to 0.36 dex (Fig. 10, p. 11; Fig. 11, p. 10).
  4. Directly fitting the real MIRI photometry removes the offset almost entirely (median \(\chi^2_\nu=1.31\), p. 9) and, crucially, lowers total inferred \(L_{\rm IR}\) for dusty galaxies (\(A_V\gtrsim1\)) by up to ~0.6 dex relative to the optical-only fit (Fig. 13, p. 11-12) — i.e., the fix is a genuine reduction in absorbed/re-emitted energy, not just a reshaping of the dust SED template, consistent with steeper SMC-like attenuation curves or complex star-dust geometries rather than simple self-shielding [§4.6, pp. 11-13].

Caveats & limitations

The authors note their MIRI photometry required an empirical 18% flux recalibration against COSMOS2025 due to point-source aperture-correction bias in extended sources [§3.3, p. 5]. Mock-SED recovery tests (Appendix D, pp. 18) show that at \(z=2.5\) MIRI broadband photometry alone cannot usefully constrain individual dust-grain parameters (\(q_{PAH}\), \(U_{min}\), \(\gamma\)) because PAH features shift out of band coverage — so the large scatter in best-fit dust emission parameters (Fig. 12, p. 12) reflects data limitations, not real diversity, as the authors state explicitly [§4.5, p. 10; §4.6, p. 13]. They explicitly weigh and disfavor a self-shielding explanation for the discrepancy because self-shielding should conserve \(L_{\rm IR}\), which contradicts the observed 0.6 dex drop [§5, p. 13]. The final sample (89 of 153 Blue Jay galaxies) excludes AGN and poorly-resolved sources, and the paper does not quantify how representative this subsample is in terms of morphology/inclination, which my own reading flags as relevant since line-of-sight geometry is central to their proposed explanation (my own observation, not stated as a limitation by the authors). Also not discussed by the authors: no independent far-IR/submm data exist to directly verify where the "missing" absorbed energy actually emerges.

Assessment

This is a focused, technically careful empirical test rather than a methods paper — strongest contribution is the new public Blue Jay MIRI catalogue and the demonstration, with a reasonably sized sample (89-111 galaxies) and clear quantitative trends, that energy balance fails systematically and predictably with \(A_V\) rather than randomly. The NIRSpec-vs-MIRI comparison (Figs. 10-11) is a nice internal consistency check that strengthens the causal story (degeneracy-breaking narrows but doesn't eliminate the gap; direct IR data does). For this researcher's interests, it's directly relevant to full-spectrum SED fitting and inferring stellar/dust properties from spectra — a useful, concrete caution that default Prospector/energy-balance LIR and SFR_IR estimates for dusty \(z\sim2\) galaxies may be biased high by up to ~0.6 dex, which matters for any work combining spectroscopic stellar-population fits with photometric SFR/dust budgets. Less directly relevant to ML/representation-learning methods, but the mock-recovery exercise (Appendix D) is a good reminder that broadband SED parameter degeneracies are a data problem, something simulation-based inference approaches could in principle probe further.

Code & data

Photometric pipeline "miriutils" (Zenodo, DOI given p. 3); Blue Jay MIRI Photometric Catalogue v2.0 (Zenodo, DOI given p. 5 and p. 12); Blue Jay photometric and spectroscopic catalogues referenced via Belli et al. 2026 (Zenodo DOIs, p. 2).

2. Beyond the blur: Radial binning trumps stellar migration in resolved star formation histories

Charlotte Olsen, Charlotte Welker, Ena Chia, Anna C Wright · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF

Why it was picked: Spatially resolved star formation histories and stellar migration effects are core to the researcher's resolved-SFH/stellar-population interests. — score 7.2 (you 8/10 · field 5/10)

TL;DR. Using the high-resolution h277 Milky-Way-analog cosmological simulation with star-particle tracking, the authors show that adaptive, S/N-driven radial binning (as used in photometric SED fitting) recovers specific star-formation-rate (sSFR) radial profiles from resolved star-formation histories (SFHs) more accurately than finer, fixed-width IFU-like bins, largely compensating for stellar migration — except in galaxies that have undergone a recent major merger [Abstract, p. 1].

Main question

Spatially resolved SFHs, inferred from integral-field spectroscopy or multi-band photometric SED fitting, are commonly used to map where and when star formation occurs within galaxies, informing "inside-out" vs "outside-in" growth/quenching scenarios [§1, p. 1]. These reconstructions implicitly assume stars observed in a spatial bin today formed there, but stellar migration ("churning" and "blurring") can move stars between radii, potentially biasing inferred SFHs [§1, pp. 1–2]. The paper asks: how much does radial migration actually bias sSFR profiles reconstructed from resolved SFHs, and can binning choices mitigate this bias, given current/upcoming survey resolution limits (e.g., LSST/Rubin)? [§1, p. 2].

Data & method

The authors use the h277 Milky-Way-mass cosmological zoom simulation (GASOLINE SPH code), chosen because it explicitly resolves non-equilibrium H\(_2\) abundance and hence a realistic cold multiphase ISM and star-formation-driven velocity dispersions, giving it an unusually realistic treatment of stellar migration compared to other simulations of similar resolution [§2.0.2, pp. 3–4]. At \(z=0\) it has virial mass \(6.8\times10^{11}\,M_\odot\), \(R_{\rm vir}=227\) kpc, \(M_*=4.1\times10^{10}\,M_\odot\), with >5 million star particles and no major merger since \(z=3\) [§2.0.1, p. 3].

They track individual star particles across four "evolutionary windows" — Quiescent (\(z=0\)), Declining (\(z=0.312\)), Star-forming (\(z=0.846\)), and Merger (\(z=1.939\)) — comparing, at lookback times \(\Delta t \approx 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5\) Gyr, "Ground Truth" sSFR (measured directly in past snapshots) against "SED-like" reconstructions (inferred from star ages in the present-day bin, assuming no migration) [§2.2–2.3, pp. 5–6; Tab. 2, p. 9]. Two binning schemes are compared: fine, fixed 1 kpc-wide shells (mimicking IFU surveys like SAMI/Hector) and variable-width bins matched to the Voronoi-cell (voxel) sizes actually measured in UVCANDELS photometric SED fitting, which grow from ~2 kpc in the core to ~8 kpc in the outskirts due to S/N requirements [Fig. 2, p. 4; Tab. 1, p. 5].

Main results

  • Median profiles are recoverable, scatter is not. Fig. 5 [p. 8] shows that outside the merger window, the median inferred sSFR radial profile matches ground truth closely at all lookback times, but the inferred scatter is systematically narrower (underestimated by \(\gtrsim30\%\)) because migration smooths out past bursts [§3, p. 6; §3.2, p. 9].
  • Variable-width (voxel-matched) bins outperform fine fixed bins. Fig. 6 [p. 11] quantifies percent errors in slope, scatter, and median sSFR versus lookback time: slope recovery is within 20% for both schemes but <12% for variable bins; variable bins reduce scatter error by 5–15 percentage points and keep mean-sSFR error <20% vs. >20% systematically for fine bins. Fig. 7 [p. 13] shows variable bins also better preserve the radial shape of normalized scatter, especially near the center and outskirts.
  • Migration is small and largely self-canceling except near mergers. Figs. 8–10 [pp. 14–17] show bin-crossing fractions stay below ~4% (up to ~13–16% near the center), with mean migration distances smaller than the (adaptive) bin widths; a bin-wise drift velocity (Eq. 2, p. 13) shows variable bins minimize net drift at all radii, and Fig. 11 [p. 18] shows the UVCANDELS voxel-size curve forms an effective upper envelope for migration distance across non-merger windows.
  • Major mergers break the method entirely. In the Merger window, spatial information is essentially lost (errors up to 100% in extent, scatter errors 60–75%) because bin-crossing fractions reach ~60% and displacements up to ~13 kpc [§3.1.4, p. 9; §4, pp. 12–13]. The authors therefore recommend against SFH-based radial-profile reconstruction for galaxies with signs of a recent (≲2 Gyr) major merger [§5.3, p. 19; Fig. 12, p. 19].

Caveats & limitations

The authors note this is a "best case scenario" limited to migration effects alone, excluding observational/modeling SED-fitting uncertainties [§2.2, p. 5]. Results derive from a single simulated galaxy (h277), argued to represent an upper envelope of migration strength due to its strong bar, clumpy transient spirals, and dynamically cold star formation [§5.2, pp. 16–18], but generalization to other morphologies/masses is not tested. Clumpy/actively star-forming systems show degraded reliability beyond ~1 Gyr lookback (errors up to 48% in sSFR, peak-location offsets of several kpc) [§3.1.3, p. 9; §6, p. 19]. My own observation: the paper doesn't discuss how robust conclusions are to the specific choice of 100 Myr SFR-averaging window, nor test other galaxy masses/redshifts/non-barred systems — all left as single-case extrapolations.

Assessment

This is a focused, well-targeted validation study rather than a broad methods paper, and its central message — bin width should scale with (and was serendipitously already tracking) migration distance — is a genuinely useful, actionable result for anyone doing resolved SFH/SED fitting with Rubin-like photometric surveys, directly relevant to inferring galaxy physical-property gradients and SFHs from spectra/photometry. The single-simulation caveat is real but mitigated by the authors' careful argument that h277 is a conservative (upper-bound) test case [§5.2]. For someone doing full-spectrum fitting or resolved stellar-population work, the key takeaway is pragmatic: migration is not the dominant systematic if S/N-driven adaptive binning is already in use, but merger-disturbed galaxies should be excluded from radial SFH analyses.

Code & data

Not mentioned. The h277 simulation itself is noted as "publicly available" (A. Brooks 2020) [§2.0.1, p. 3], but no code repository or specific data-release link for this paper's analysis is given in the text.

3. Black holes in little red and blue dots drive no superwinds in the first 1.5 billion years

Mainak Singha, Yurina Nakazato, Wei Leong Tee · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF

Why it was picked: Large JWST/NIRSpec spectroscopic sample (80k spectra) testing black-hole feedback in early galaxies is a strong spectroscopy-driven galaxy evolution result. — score 7.0 (you 7/10 · field 7/10)

TL;DR. Searching 67 compact, high-redshift "little red dot" (LRD) and "little blue dot" (LBD) AGN candidates from z~5-13 for ionized outflows, the authors find zero [O III] winds and bound (sub-escape-velocity) broad-line and absorbing gas, in sharp contrast to local AGN — implying these early black holes grow without the wind feedback that galaxy-formation models invoke to regulate their hosts [p. 1].

Main question

The paper asks whether accreting black holes in the first 1.5 billion years (z≳4) drive the superwinds that theoretical models (e.g. Silk & Rees) require to couple black-hole growth to host-galaxy regulation and to the local \(M_\bullet\)–\(\sigma_\star\) relation [p. 1]. This matters because LRDs have black-hole masses that lie above the local black-hole–stellar-mass relation despite being X-ray and radio weak, raising the question of whether they are powered by super-Eddington accretion (which should launch powerful winds) and whether any feedback is regulating their growth at all [p. 1].

Data & method

The sample is drawn from 80,367 NIRSpec spectra in the DAWN JWST Archive, selecting 334 LRD and 1,927 LBD targets via continuum shape (V-shaped PRISM spectra or published candidacy for LRDs; compact blue/flat sources for LBDs) [p. 1, Tab. 1 p. 2]. Of these, 95 LRDs and 52 LBDs enter the broad-Balmer-line analysis, and 67 have [O III] grating spectra deep enough for a wind search [p. 1, Tab. 1]. The core method fits each [O III] spectrum with and without a second kinematic component, applying strict significance and blueshift cuts (Methods, p. 9), then calibrates detectability via synthetic-wind injection/recovery into the real spectra. Local comparison samples (9,682 type 1 AGN from BAT AGN Spectroscopic Survey, SDSS, and the ALPAKA catalogue) are passed through the same pipeline to set empirical wind-detection expectations split by Eddington ratio [§ "Local AGN," p. 2]. Complementary diagnostics probe broad-line-region kinematics (Hα centroids/extra components) and nuclear sightlines (Balmer absorption troughs), and the authors directly test published super-Eddington "fountain," black-hole-star, and quasi-star spectral models against the data [p. 2–4].

Main results

  1. No [O III] winds detected. Among 67 LRDs/LBDs, zero wind candidates are found, against lower-limit expectations of 7.2 (sub-Eddington-like) and 7.5 (super-Eddington-like) detections based on local AGN wind fractions of 31% and 49%; the chance of seeing zero by chance is \(4\times10^{-4}\) and \(3\times10^{-4}\) respectively (Fig. 1, p. 3). The 95%-confidence upper limits on wind incidence (for winds <1,500 km/s) are 13% and 19%, versus 31%/49% locally [p. 2].
  2. Broad-line and absorbing gas is bound, not escaping. Broad Hα is centred on the narrow lines (median offset +0.9%) with symmetric stacked profiles, unlike the local blue-wing-excess stack (Fig. 2, p. 4). Balmer absorbers in 14 LRDs/1 LBD move at only 0.001–0.08 of the escape speed (Fig. 3, p. 5) — well below the 0.34–1.04 \(v_\mathrm{esc}\) predicted by super-Eddington "fountain" models.
  3. Published super-Eddington models fail the spectra. None of the eight published fountain-model spectra simultaneously reproduces the observed Hα strength and symmetry (Fig. 4, p. 6); photon-trapping disc simulations still predict winds that are excluded; and blackbody/case-B photoionization tests of 68 LRDs show the reddened "photosphere" in quasi-star/black-hole-star models cannot supply enough ionizing photons (power deficit factor ~1.9–10×) nor match required densities or Hα wing widths (predicted 3,200–18,300 km/s vs. observed median 920 km/s) (Fig. 5, p. 7).
  4. Reanalysis of individual outflow/jet claims. The strongest published single-object outflow claim (GLIMPSED-329380) is reinterpreted as a spatially offset (1.4 kpc), non-nuclear feature rather than a genuine wind (Extended Data Fig. 1, Tab. 1, p. 10); radio stacking limits imply jet power below ~2% of bolometric luminosity, ruling out powerful jets too [p. 4–5].

Caveats & limitations

The authors note the search cannot detect winds broader than 1,500 km/s (only ~3% of local winds, and recovery tests show these are poorly recovered even when injected) [p. 5], does not trace hot, neutral, or molecular outflow phases requiring X-ray/sodium/submillimetre data [p. 5], and only applies to sources bright enough for grating spectroscopy [p. 5]. They emphasize their limits are therefore conservative lower bounds on non-detection significance. My own observations: the sample, while large for this population, still rests on uncertain redshift-dependent selection functions (V-shape criteria, literature matching) and on black-hole mass/Eddington-ratio estimates from virial Balmer-line masses whose reliability for LRDs is itself contested elsewhere in the literature the paper cites (e.g., refs 11, 18, 24) — a systematic bias there could affect how the local comparison samples are reweighted.

Assessment

This is a carefully quantified null result built on real statistical power (injection-recovery calibration, multiple local comparison samples, multiple independent kinematic diagnostics converging on the same conclusion), which makes it considerably more convincing than a typical single-object non-detection claim. For this researcher's interests, it's a strong example of spectroscopy-driven inference about galaxy-regulation physics at the AGN–host interface, directly relevant to feedback/quenching questions even though it concerns the earliest, not stellar-population, side of the field — it doesn't touch full-spectrum fitting or stellar population synthesis but is highly relevant to anyone tracking how JWST spectroscopy is reshaping black-hole–galaxy coevolution models. The "feedback-free" growth phase proposed here, with later wind-driven settling onto scaling relations, is a falsifiable prediction (rising wind incidence from the LRD epoch to cosmic noon) worth watching for follow-up work.

Code & data

Source-by-source measurements, injection–recovery results, and source identifiers are on Zenodo (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24533555); the fitting, injection-recovery, and figure scripts are at the same Zenodo DOI [p. 13]. JWST spectra are public via the DAWN JWST Archive; SDSS spectra via SDSS DR17 [p. 13].

4. Curvature Mismatch and Local Errors in Galaxy Property Estimation

Austin Lutterbach, Angus Hoy, Jasper Chong, Aman Kumar, Michael J. Smith · cs.LG · astro-ph.IM · arXiv · PDF · 💻 code/data · cross

Why it was picked: Directly examines linear-probe errors on galaxy embeddings from pretrained encoders, squarely in the ML-representation-learning-on-galaxies interest. — score 6.5 (you 7/10 · field 5/10)

TL;DR. The authors show that linear probes fit to pretrained galaxy-image embeddings inherit curvature from the embedding manifold's geometry, and that this "probe-facing curvature" can help or hurt local prediction accuracy depending on whether it matches the true curvature of the target label; correcting it locally raises held-out local \(R^2\) by 0.006–0.010 on galaxy data [Abstract, p. 1].

Main question

Linear probes are the standard tool for reading physical quantities (magnitude, redshift, stellar mass, morphology) out of frozen representations learned by pretrained encoders, but aggregate scores like a single global \(R^2\) don't explain where and why a probe fails locally [§1, p. 1]. The paper asks whether the curved geometry of the embedding manifold itself — rather than information simply missing from the representation — is a distinct, identifiable source of local probe error, and whether that geometric error can be measured and corrected [§1, pp. 1–2].

Data & method

The main dataset is 86,471 galaxies from the AstroPT galaxy set [Smith et al., 2024], with catalogue labels r-band magnitude, photometric redshift, smooth fraction and stellar mass, embedded with a supervised ImageNet-21k ViT-B/16 plus four other encoders (DINOv3 ViT-B/16, CLIP ViT-B, ConvNeXt-B, ViT-L) from the Platonic Universe release [§2, p. 2]. Embeddings are unit-normalized onto a sphere. To estimate the manifold's local curvature (the "second fundamental form," \(II\)), they train a small auto-encoder decoder per encoder and use automatic differentiation to compute the Jacobian, metric and normal projector at each point, validated against a synthetic manifold with known closed-form curvature (direction cosine 0.999, magnitude ratio 1.00) [§2, p. 2; App. A, p. 6]. They then define the probe's own "probe-facing curvature" \(K=\langle w_N, II\rangle\) [Eq. 1, p. 3], split it into an in-sphere "shape" term \(K_S\) and a radial "sphere" term \(K_{\mathrm{sph}}\) [Eq. 2, p. 3], and derive a local error expansion [Eq. 3, p. 3] in which the squared mismatch between probe curvature \(K\) and label curvature \(H\) (denoted \(\Delta\)) contributes at order \(s^4\) in patch scale \(s\). Ridge probes are fit out-of-fold at a pre-registered penalty \(\alpha=100\) and a per-label/encoder tuned \(\alpha^*\), and local \(R^2\) is scored inside 512 anchors' 2,048-galaxy neighbourhoods [§5, p. 3].

Main results

  1. Keeping vs. removing the inherited bend. Scaling the probe's curvature term by \(t\) (t=1 keeps it, t=0 removes it) shows \(t=1\) beats \(t=0\) at 79–99% of anchors (93–100% with the tuned probe) [§5, p. 4]. Fig. 1 [p. 3] visualizes this: the probe's own inherited curvature peaks at \(t^*=1\), a distant anchor's curvature peaks near \(t^*=1/2\), and a rotated or random curvature direction peaks near \(t^*=0\) — showing the gain is tied to the actual local geometry, not an artifact of least-squares fitting.
  2. Alignment matters more where curvatures disagree. Interventions (removing curvature) help more in the top-fifth most misaligned regions between probe and label curvature (5.81% of the time) than in the top-fifth most aligned regions (2.03% of the time) [§5, p. 4; Tab. 3, App. C, p. 9].
  3. Local bend correction. Fitting a small per-neighbourhood ridge correction that moves the probe's weight along the decoder's 136 local bending directions raises held-out local \(R^2\) by 0.0059–0.0095 on ViT-B [§5, p. 4], with larger curvature-driven gains where probe and label curvature are less aligned (rank-partial Spearman −0.17 to −0.28 for three of four labels). The same correction generalizes to a non-astronomical dataset (QM9 molecules via MoLFormer-XL), where gains are larger (0.044–0.049) [Tab. 4, App. D, p. 9].
  4. Practical catalogue implication. Grouping galaxies by neighbourhood brightness, the faintest third has local \(R^2\) up to 0.22 lower than the brightest third for three of four labels, but most of this gap traces to label noise rather than probe/geometry error [§5, p. 4; App. B, p. 8].

Caveats & limitations

The authors note the study covers one galaxy sample, four labels and five encoders [§6, p. 4]; roughly half the estimated label-Hessian norm is noise floor rather than signal [App. B, p. 7]; per-anchor residual curvature cannot reliably rank individual objects (split-half \(\rho\approx0.33\)) [§6, p. 4]; the intervention modifies a local surrogate, not the encoder itself; and mismatch/alignment definitions were chosen post hoc after the pre-registered mean-curvature analysis, making the intervention results exploratory [§6, pp. 4–5]. Overlapping anchor neighbourhoods are treated as independent in permutation tests, which the authors flag as a simplification [§6, p. 5]. Not discussed by the authors, and worth flagging myself: all curvature estimates depend on a single decoder architecture/training protocol per encoder (seed 0), and no uncertainty on the decoder itself is propagated; the catalogue labels (especially photo-z and stellar mass) carry their own model-dependent systematics that are treated purely as "noise" rather than potential bias.

Assessment

This is a methods/diagnostics paper aimed at practitioners who use linear probes on foundation-model embeddings — directly relevant to stellar-population and survey ML work that reads physical parameters out of representation-learning pipelines (e.g. AstroPT-style self-supervised models). Its core contribution is conceptual: decomposing probe error into a geometric (curvature-mismatch) term with a clean, validated estimator, rather than new predictive accuracy. The numbers are modest (local \(R^2\) gains of ~0.006–0.01 on galaxies) and the authors are commendably careful about noise floors and pre-registration, which makes we trust the direction of the effect even though its practical size is small. For someone doing full-spectrum fitting or stellar-mass/SFH inference from spectra with deep-learning encoders, the actionable takeaway is the three rules in §5 [p. 4]: tune the regularization penalty carefully, evaluate accuracy by local neighbourhood rather than only globally, and recognize that probe errors concentrated in sparse/faint regions of embedding space may reflect label noise rather than representation quality. It's a useful diagnostic lens rather than a new model, and its generalization to QM9 molecular embeddings is a nice (if limited) hint that the geometric framing is architecture-agnostic.

Code & data

Code, records and notebooks are at https://github.com/alutterb/galaxy-curvature [§1, p. 2]. Data are the AstroPT galaxy set [Smith et al., 2024] embeddings from the Platonic Universe release [UniverseTBD et al., 2025], and QM9 [Ramakrishnan et al., 2014] for the molecular appendix [§2, p. 2; App. D, p. 9].

5. Less massive black holes in faint hosts: SED fitting of Little Red Dots within the Black Hole Star scenario

Haitong Liu, Lulu Fan, Yunkun Han, Yibin Luo · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF

The paper asks whether JWST's 'little red dots' can be explained by the black-hole-star (BH) scenario, where a super-Eddington black hole sits in a dense gaseous envelope. Fitting 22 LRDs at \(z\sim4\)–9.5 with a host+BH Cloudy/BayeSED3 model, they find faint low-mass hosts (\(M_*\sim10^{8.7}M_\odot\)) with moderately overmassive black holes (\(M_{BH}\sim10^{6.4}M_\odot\)), reducing the LRD number-density tension to a factor of a few.

Why you might care: Relevant mainly as SED-fitting methodology (BayeSED3, photoionization modeling) applied to a hot topic, though it's AGN/SED rather than core stellar-population spectroscopy. (score 5.2 (you 5/10 · field 6/10))

Abstract The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has revealed a population of compact sources known as ``little red dots'' (LRDs), characterized by a distinctive V-shaped spectral energy distribution (SED) from UV to optical wavelengths. The physical nature of these sources remains debated. The ``black hole star'' (BH*) scenario, wherein a super-Eddington accreting black hole is embedded in a dense gaseous envelope, has been proposed to explain them. We assemble a sample of 22 LRDs at $4.0 \lesssim z \lesssim 9.5$ from the ASTRODEEP-JWST database. We construct a composite ``host galaxy + BH*'' model using the \texttt{Cloudy} photoionization code, and perform SED fitting with \textsc{BayeSED3}. Our modeling reproduces the observed V-shaped features: the UV continuum blueward of the Balmer break is well reproduced by stellar emission within this decomposition, while the optical regime is dominated by the BH* component. Our fits reveal that LRDs are black holes hosted by faint, low-mass galaxies with median stellar and black hole masses of $M_* = 10^{8.7}\,M_\odot$ and $M_{\rm BH} = 10^{6.4}\,M_\odot$, respectively. These black holes are moderately overmassive relative to local scaling relations but less extreme than broad-line estimates. The median bolometric luminosity $L_{\rm BH^*} \sim 10^{44.9}\,\rm erg\,s^{-1}$ mitigates the reported number density tension from 1--2 orders of magnitude to a factor of a few. The host galaxies lie near the high-redshift star-forming main sequence, and stellar extinction correlates positively with star formation rate (SFR). The BH* scenario provides a self-consistent paradigm for LRDs, offering insights into the rapid growth of supermassive black hole seeds and their co-evolution with host galaxies during the cosmic dawn.

6. When precision exceeds fidelity: systematic error quantification for inference with misspecified simulators

Abstract As scientific datasets grow in complexity and precision, the primary source of uncertainty in parameter inference shifts from measurement noise to systematic errors within the simulators themselves. Abstract We report the discovery of J0930+2902, the first carbon-enhanced metal-poor star with s-process enrichment (CEMP-s) identified in the GD-1 stellar stream, which is widely believed to originate from the tidal disruption of an old, metal-poor globular cluster. Using high-resolution Subaru/HDS spectroscopy, we present a comprehensive chemical abundance analysis of this star. Stellar atmospheric parameters were re-derived using Gaia photometry and parallaxes, combined with an iterative non-local thermodynamic equilibrium (NLTE) analysis of Fe lines. We determine abundances for 22 elements, including NLTE analyses of C, Na, Mg, Ca, Ti, Cr, Mn, Co, Sr, Y, Zr, Ba, and Eu. J0930+2902 exhibits pronounced enhancements of C, N, Na, and s-process elements, together with a low $^{12}$C/$^{13}$C ratio of 7.4, consistent with enrichment through binary mass transfer from a former asymptotic giant branch (AGB) companion. Large radial-velocity variations and a significant near-ultraviolet excess independently indicate the presence of a white dwarf companion, providing strong support for this scenario. Comparisons with FRUITY AGB nucleosynthesis models show that the residual neutron-capture abundance pattern is best reproduced by a low-mass AGB companion with an initial mass of approximately 3.0 $M_{\odot}$. Its enhancements of C, N, Na, and s-process elements make it chemically distinct from the other confirmed GD-1 members. These results identify J0930+2902 as a fossil record of binary mass transfer preserved in the ancient GD-1 stellar stream, demonstrating that binary interaction can leave long-lived chemical signatures in accreted stellar populations.
