1. FastSpecFit: Spectrophotometric Modeling of 38 Million Extragalactic Targets from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument Second Data Release

John Moustakas, Jeremy Buhler, Dirk Scholte, Jessica Aguilar, Steven Ahlen, Abhijeet Anand et al. · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF · 💻 code/data · 💻 code/data

Why it was picked: FastSpecFit DESI DR2 full-spectrum fitting catalog for 38M galaxies is exactly core-topic: stellar pop synthesis, SFR/mass/dust from spectra at survey scale. — score 8.5 (you 9/10 · field 7/10)

TL;dr. FastSpecFit is a new open-source forward-modeling code that jointly fits DESI spectra and broadband photometry for speed and scale, and the paper describes its algorithms and presents the resulting value-added catalog (VAC) of spectrophotometric and physical properties for 38 million DESI DR2 extragalactic targets at \(0<z\lesssim4\) [p. 1].

Main question

The paper asks how to extract stellar-population, kinematic, and emission-line properties from tens of millions of DESI spectra—fast, robustly, and uniformly across the enormous diversity of DESI targets (quiescent galaxies, star-forming galaxies, and AGN/quasars)—without resorting to class-specific pipelines [§1, p. 4]. The authors argue that existing full-spectrum-fitting and SED-fitting codes (pPXF, STARLIGHT, CIGALE, Prospector, etc.) were not designed for this scale, and none exploit DESI's resolution matrix, which preserves uncorrelated pixel statistics [§1, p. 5].

Data & method

FastSpecFit forward-models each DESI spectrum (3600–9824 Å, \(R\approx2000\)–5200) jointly with grz plus WISE W1–W4 photometry from the Legacy Surveys DR9, convolving stellar-population-synthesis (SPS) templates with each fiber's resolution matrix rather than resampling the data [§2, p. 7; §3, p. 10]. SPS templates come from FSPS/C3K, MIST isochrones, and a Chabrier IMF, built as five non-parametric "continuity" age bins (0–30 Myr through 11.6–13.7 Gyr) at fixed solar metallicity [Fig. 1, p. 11; §3.1, p. 11]. The continuum fit (template coefficients, dust optical depth \(\tau_V\), velocity dispersion \(\sigma_\star\)) uses a variable-projection scheme—Brent minimization over \(\tau_V\) with an inner non-negative least-squares solve for template weights [Eq. 13–14, p. 21]—while emission lines (46 transitions, Table 1, [p. 13]) are fit afterward with log-Gaussian profiles deconvolved against the resolution matrix to recover flux correctly even for lines narrower than a pixel [§3.5.1, p. 23]. Narrow and broad Balmer components are tested via four statistical criteria [§3.5.2, p. 27]. Parameter uncertainties come from 50 Monte Carlo noise realizations per object rather than posterior sampling [§3.7, p. 33].

Main results

  1. Catalog scale and speed. The DR2 VAC covers 38,245,183 objects (Table 5, [p. 42]), with a median single-core runtime of 4.8 s/object (including 50 Monte Carlo draws) on NERSC Perlmutter [§5.3, p. 47]; without Monte Carlo, a typical fit takes \(\approx0.1\) s [p. 1].
  2. Emission-line validation. Fig. 6 [p. 48] compares fastspec line fluxes to the emlinefit afterburner and an independent Zou et al. CIGALE-based VAC: [O II] agrees to \(-0.006\pm0.022\) dex with emlinefit, while H\(\alpha\)/H\(\beta\)/[O III] show offsets of \(+0.029\pm0.020\), \(+0.085\pm0.067\), and \(-0.115\pm0.033\) dex respectively, attributed to emlinefit's simpler local-continuum treatment [p. 49].
  3. Kinematics and masses. Fig. 7 [p. 50] shows \(\sigma_\star\) agrees with independent pPXF fits (C. E. Ross et al. 2025) to within \(15.9\pm12.9\) km s\(^{-1}\) (pull \(1.06\pm0.82\)). Fig. 8 [p. 51] shows stellar masses are systematically \(0.15\)–\(0.30\) dex higher than three CIGALE-based catalogs (NMAD \(0.09\)–\(0.17\) dex for BGS/LRG, rising to \(\approx0.4\) dex for ELGs), consistent with known non-parametric-vs-parametric SFH differences [p. 52].
  4. Worked examples. Three fits illustrate the dynamic range: a \(z=0.0813\) BGS galaxy with \(\sigma_\star=287\pm11\) km s\(^{-1}\) and a type-2 Seyfert BPT classification [Fig. 3, p. 37]; a \(z=0.9449\) ELG with resolved [O II] doublet giving \(n_e=275^{+94}_{-89}\) cm\(^{-3}\) [Fig. 4, p. 39]; and a \(z=2.0731\) QSO where the purely stellar continuum model is explicitly flagged as unreliable for stellar-population parameters [Fig. 5, p. 40–41].

Caveats & limitations

The authors state several explicit limitations: the templates contain no AGN continuum or AGN emission lines, so continuum-derived properties (mass, SFR, age, dust) "should not be used for QSOs" [§4.3, p. 40]; metallicity, the attenuation-curve slope, and IR dust parameters are fixed by default [§3.1, p. 5]; the aperture correction assumes the fiber spectral shape is wavelength-independent and representative of the whole galaxy, a simplification the authors acknowledge is imperfect for galaxies with radial gradients [§3.4.2, p. 19]; and a velocity-dispersion definition bug (\({\Delta}v\) not being a rest-frame quantity) is noted mid-text as a known issue to be fixed in the next code version [footnote, p. 23]. The BGS example also shows the well-known age–dust–metallicity degeneracy inflating \(\tau_V\) [§4.1, p. 36]. An un-discussed limitation (my own observation) is that validation samples are dominated by SV3/bright-dark and main-survey subsets, and it's unclear how representative these are of the full DR2 depth/S/N distribution.

Assessment

This is primarily an infrastructure/methods paper rather than a new physical result, but the infrastructure is significant: it is the measurement backbone enabling dozens of downstream DESI papers (dwarf AGN, metal-poor galaxies, void galaxies, mass-metallicity relation) already cited [§1, p. 6]. The validation is reasonably thorough and honest about discrepancies (e.g., the unexplained \(\sigma_\star\) offset, [p. 50]) rather than overclaiming agreement. For this researcher's interests, FastSpecFit is directly relevant as a full-spectrum-fitting / SED-fitting tool at survey scale, and the forward-modeling-with-resolution-matrix and VARPRO tricks are useful engineering patterns for any large-spectroscopic-survey pipeline (4MOST, WEAVE, PFS). It is not an ML paper per se—no neural network or representation-learning component is used for the modeling itself (QuasarNet is used only as an external redshift afterburner, [§5.1, p. 42])—so it's more a data-product/pipeline paper than a new ML method, but it is a key value-added dataset for ML-on-spectra and stellar-population-inference applications.

Code & data

Code: FastSpecFit documentation at https://fastspecfit.readthedocs.io/en/latest [p. 5, footnote]; also linked repos for FSPS fork, python-fsps, and C3K templates [p. 11, footnotes]. Data: DR2 Loa/v2.0 VAC to be released with DESI DR2 (scheduled first part of 2027), with catalog structure and access documented at https://fastspecfit.readthedocs.io/en/latest/loa.html [§5.1, p. 42–44].

2. Spectro-Spatial Survey Fusion

Georgios N. Vassilakis, Miles Cranmer, Vasily Belokurov · astro-ph.CO · astro-ph.IM · arXiv · PDF · 💻 code/data

Why it was picked: Novel ML framework fusing imaging and spectroscopy (SDSS/DESI/HSC) into a shared representation, directly matches ML-on-spectra and multimodal interests. — score 7.5 (you 8/10 · field 6/10)

TL;DR. This methods paper introduces Spectro-Spatial Survey Fusion, a framework that jointly fits a single continuous spectral surface-brightness field to imaging and fiber spectra via each instrument's forward operator, tested on 400 SDSS \(ugriz\) exposures and 800 fiber spectra over an 84′×84′ COSMOS patch [Abstract, p. 1].

Main question

The paper asks how imaging and spectroscopy of the same sky—typically processed separately with different catalogs, PSFs, and resolutions—can be fit as a single, continuous model so that information from one instrument constrains predictions for another [§1, p. 1]. This matters because catalog matching can disagree on source identities in crowded fields, and coaddition retains a fixed bandpass/PSF and cannot ingest a fiber spectrum directly [§1, p. 1]. The authors frame their approach relative to forward-modeling tools (The Tractor, The Farmer, scarlet) and hyperspectral/IFU fusion work, but note survey fiber spectroscopy is sparse relative to imaged area, which prior fusion work did not address [§1, p. 2].

Data & method

The sky is represented as a spectral surface-brightness field \(F_\lambda(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)\) [Eq. 1, p. 2], written as a sum of \(J\) anisotropic 2D Gaussian components, each with an amplitude and a spectrum built from a shared, learned dictionary of \(K=32\) non-negative templates (96 log-wavelength knots, 2980–11230 Å) [Eq. 3–4, §2.2, p. 3]. Gaussians retain their form under affine transforms and convolution, enabling analytic PSF convolution for imaging; fiber-aperture integration uses numerical quadrature [§2.2–2.3, pp. 2–4]. The model is adapted from 3D Gaussian splatting (Kerbl et al. 2023), borrowing adaptive density control (growth/splitting/pruning) to let the Gaussian population evolve [§3.4, p. 6]. Data: 82 SDSS fields (410 exposures, 2000–2001) and 889 spectra (557 SDSS + 332 BOSS, 2000–2011) over COSMOS [§3.1, p. 5]. 400 exposures and 800 spectra are fit (the "canonical fit"); 10 exposures (2/band) and 89 spectra are withheld for testing, with no object catalog used to seed sources—components are initialized from image peaks [§3.1–3.2, p. 5]. The joint objective sums inverse-variance-weighted squared residuals over pixels and spectral bins, with spectroscopy weighted by a fixed hyperparameter \(\gamma_{\rm spec}=5\) [Eq. 9, §3.3, p. 6]. Fitting used Adam with sparse updates, 11 "image epochs" (~960,000 updates), 8 hours on one NVIDIA B200 GPU, ending with 2.86 million Gaussian components [§3.4, §4.3, pp. 6, 8].

Main results

  1. Fit quality on training data: Fig. 5 [p. 9] compares the observed and fitted SDSS \(g\)-band mosaic across the full field and a 7′ zoom; sky-pixel MSP (mean squared pull, essentially \(\chi^2\)/datum) is 0.98–1.06 across the five bands, and summed flux in source-selected pixels is within 0.4–1.7% of observed [§4.1, p. 8]. Fig. 6 [p. 10] shows joint image+spectrum predictions for six objects (galaxies, stars, a quasar); Table 1 [p. 8] shows 96% of the 800 fitted spectra agree in integrated flux to within 10%, with median flux ratios ~0.98–0.99 across brightness terciles, though median MSP rises from 0.99 (faintest) to 4.37 (brightest tercile).
  2. Spectroscopy improves predictions beyond fitted fibers: Fig. 7 [p. 11] shows that, for 89 withheld fiber positions, joint (image+spectra) fits have lower MSP than images-only fits for 88/89 spectra across three random seeds (median MSP 6.01 vs. 98.5) [§5.1.1, p. 10].
  3. Generalization to new instruments, zero-shot: Without refitting, the SDSS-trained field predicts HSC \(g\)-band images (Fig. 9, p. 12) and DESI spectra (Table 2, p. 13; Fig. 10, p. 14) that never entered the fit. For galaxies with no fitted SDSS fiber nearby, median HSC flux ratio in 6″-radius apertures is 1.01 (49 galaxies), and median DESI integrated-flux ratio is 0.986 (80 galaxies) [§5.2.1–5.2.2, pp. 11–13]; DESI shape-error (residual spectral structure) is lower for galaxies/quasars/stars that do have a fitted SDSS spectrum nearby (e.g., 5.6% vs. 9.9% for galaxies) [Tab. 2, p. 13].
  4. Withheld whole exposures: Fig. 8 [p. 12] shows prediction of a fully withheld \(r\)-band exposure reproduces broad spatial structure, but flux can be biased (ratios 0.45–1.16 across the 10 withheld exposures in regions lacking same-band coverage) [§5.1.2, p. 11].

Caveats & limitations

The authors note: (i) narrow spectral features (absorption troughs, emission lines) are systematically underpredicted/smoothed, partly attributable to coarse template knot spacing (~84 Å at 6000 Å) versus few-Å line-spread-function widths [§6, p. 14; §4.2, p. 8]; (ii) sub-PSF spatial structure is only weakly constrained and "ill-posed," with elongated Gaussian "streak" artifacts resembling known Gaussian-splatting failure modes (Fig. 11a, p. 15); (iii) uncorrected survey background errors can be absorbed into the field as spurious extended structure (Fig. 11b, p. 15); (iv) bright stars without fitted SDSS spectra show a systematic DESI flux deficit (median ratio 0.814, worsening with brightness), possibly linked to saturated/masked cores [§6, p. 14]; (v) no calibrated per-point uncertainties are yet provided for field queries, since component degeneracies complicate error estimation [§6, p. 15]. My own observations: the HSC/DESI "zero-shot" demonstrations use small, brightness-selected samples (e.g., 49–80 objects per class) rather than full populations, and are explicitly described by the authors as "deliberately bright" test sets [§5.2.2, p. 13], so generalization to faint/typical survey objects is untested.

Assessment

This is a methods/proof-of-concept paper, not a stellar-population or galaxy-evolution result — no physical parameters (ages, metallicities, SFHs) are extracted, and the "spectral dictionary" templates are explicitly non-physical shared basis functions, not stellar population models [§2.2, p. 3]. For this researcher's interests, the direct scientific payoff is limited, but the framework is relevant as an alternative representation-learning paradigm for survey data: it's a continuous, shared-latent-field approach (conceptually adjacent to self-supervised/multimodal representation learning across imaging and spectra) that could eventually feed into stellar-population pipelines if combined with physical spectral templates and extended to surveys like DESI/4MOST/WEAVE at scale. The demonstrated zero-shot transfer across instruments (HSC, DESI) without refitting is the most novel and convincing piece, given it generalizes to a genuinely held-out instrument response. However, the test region is small (84′×84′, one field), spectral line fidelity is weak, and no uncertainty quantification exists yet — both flagged candidly by the authors — so this reads as an early-stage proof of concept rather than a survey-ready tool.

Code & data

Code: a frozen snapshot at https://github.com/GeorgeVassilakis/survey-fusion-paper-code, described as "unmaintained," excluding fitted checkpoints and the complete HSC/DESI analysis workflows [Data Availability, p. 16]. Data: SDSS, HSC PDR3, DESI DR1, and Gaia DR2 are stated to be available through their respective public archives [Data Availability, p. 16].

3. The Role of Galactic Shear in Regulating Local Star Formation Efficiency: A Combined MaNGA DynPop and ALMaQUEST Study

Kai Zhu, Shude Mao, Michele Cappellari, Sara L. Ellison, Tao Jing, Lihwai Lin et al. · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF

Why it was picked: MaNGA spectroscopy + dynamics driving local star-formation efficiency is squarely in core galaxy spectroscopy/evolution interests. — score 7.2 (you 8/10 · field 5/10)

TL;DR

Using spatially resolved Hα and CO data from MaNGA/ALMaQUEST combined with dynamical modelling (MaNGA DynPop), the authors find that galactic shear (quantified by the Oort parameter \(A_{\rm Oort}\)) is the single dynamical property most predictive of local star formation efficiency (SFE), suppressing it on average, but the effect is weak, varies strongly from galaxy to galaxy, and explains only the radial trend — azimuthal scatter in SFE is left to cloud-scale physics [Abstract, p. 1].

Main question

The paper asks what regulates the local star formation efficiency, \(\Sigma_{\rm SFE} \equiv \Sigma_{\rm SFR}/\Sigma_{\rm H_2}\), within star-forming disks — specifically, whether galactic shear suppresses SFE (by stabilizing giant molecular clouds, GMCs, against collapse) or instead enhances it (via shear-driven cloud–cloud collisions), and how shear's role compares to other candidate dynamical regulators such as mass surface density, Toomre stability, and mid-plane pressure [§1, p. 1-2]. This matters because star-formation quenching mechanisms are debated, and "morphological/gravitational quenching" — where a galaxy retains molecular gas but fails to convert it to stars efficiently — is one proposed pathway for which the physical driver is unclear [§1, p. 2].

Data & method

The sample combines IFU data from the MaNGA survey (DR SDSS-IV, ~10,000 galaxies) with CO(1-0) maps from ALMaQUEST (46 galaxies) plus its extension (20 more, mostly mergers) [§2.1, p. 2-3]. Dynamical properties (circular velocity, Oort parameter \(A_{\rm Oort}\), Toomre \(Q\), total/stellar mass surface density, mid-plane pressure \(P_{\rm DE}\)) come from Jeans Anisotropic Modelling (JAM) in the MaNGA DynPop project [§2.1, p. 3]. After requiring reliable dynamical fits (Qual \(\geq 1\)) and removing one poorly-constrained galaxy, the final sample is 29 galaxies, predominantly disk-dominated (median bulge-to-total ratio 0.186) and mostly central galaxies in halos of \(10^{12}\)–\(10^{13}\,M_\odot\) [§2.1, p. 3-4; Fig. 1, p. 5]. Star-forming regions are selected via BPT classification, yielding 7925 Voronoi bins satisfying S/N cuts on Hα, Hβ, [O III], [N II] and CO [§2.2, p. 6]. \(\Sigma_{\rm SFR}\) comes from dust-corrected Hα (Eqs. 2-5, p. 4), \(\Sigma_{\rm H_2}\) from CO with a fixed \(\alpha_{\rm CO}=4.3\,M_\odot\,({\rm K\,km\,s^{-1}\,pc^2})^{-1}\) (tested against a metallicity-dependent version) [§2.2, p. 5]. The key analysis tool is a random forest regression (scikit-learn, 70/30 train/test split) predicting \(\Sigma_{\rm SFE}\) (and separately \(\Sigma_{\rm SFR}\)) from six local dynamical/structural quantities plus a random control variable, with feature importance and permutation importance used to rank predictors [§3.2, p. 9-10].

Main results

  1. Shear is the top-ranked SFE predictor, but weakly so. Fig. 3 [p. 11] shows \(A_{\rm Oort}\) has the highest median feature importance for predicting \(\Sigma_{\rm SFE}\), anti-correlated with \(\rho_{\rm Spearman}=-0.28\), ahead of \(P_{\rm DE}\), \(\Sigma_{\rm total}\), \(Q_{\rm Toomre}\), and \(\Sigma_*\); overall model performance is only moderate (\(R^2=0.437\)) [§3.2, p. 10]. Rankings are described as robust across many sensitivity tests (different \(\alpha_{\rm CO}\), PSF assumptions, CO-based rotation curves, BPT selection, inner/outer radius splits) summarized in Table 1 [p. 13], though normalizing by within-galaxy median flips the top rank to \(P_{\rm DE}\), with \(A_{\rm Oort}\) still non-negligible [§3.2, p. 12].
  2. The shear–SFE relation is not universal across galaxies. Of 27 usable galaxies, 18 show statistically significant (p<0.05) \(\Sigma_{\rm SFE}\)–\(A_{\rm Oort}\) correlations; 14 of these are anti-correlations (some stronger than the ensemble trend) but 4 are positive, shown galaxy-by-galaxy in Fig. 4 [p. 14] — direct evidence of galaxy-to-galaxy diversity in how shear affects SFE.
  3. Gas density modulates the shear effect. Fig. 5 [p. 15] splits bins by \(\Sigma_{\rm H_2}\) or gas fraction \(f_{\rm H_2}\): gas-poor regions show steeper, more negative SFE–shear slopes than gas-rich regions (e.g., \(\rho_{\rm Spearman}=-0.33\) vs. \(-0.22\) for the \(\Sigma_{\rm H_2}\) split), consistent with competition between shear-driven cloud stabilization (dominant at low gas density) and shear-driven cloud–cloud collisions (which become more important as gas density rises) [§3.3, p. 13-16].
  4. Azimuthal SFE scatter is not explained by the axisymmetric dynamical quantities. After removing the radial trend tied to \(A_{\rm Oort}\) (by computing residuals at fixed shear), Fig. 6 [p. 16] shows only weak residual correlations with \(P_{\rm DE}\) (r=0.24), \(\sigma_{\rm H_2}\) (r=0.15), and essentially none with \(\Sigma_*\) or \(Q_{\rm Toomre}\) — implying the remaining azimuthal variation is likely governed by cloud-scale ISM processes below the ~kpc resolution, or by non-axisymmetric dynamics (bars, spiral arms) not captured by the axisymmetric \(A_{\rm Oort}\) [§3.4, p. 15-17].

Caveats & limitations

The authors note: the sample is only 29 galaxies, limiting statistical power for galaxy-to-galaxy or gas-density-dependent analyses [§3.3, p. 16]; the random forest explains only a modest fraction of SFE variance (\(R^2=0.437\)) and feature importance reflects correlation, not causation [§3.2, p. 10]; the fixed/narrow range of gas surface densities sampled prevents the gas-density modulation result from reaching clear statistical significance [§3.3, p. 16]; alternative photometric \(\Sigma_*\) tracers (WISE/Spitzer) could not be tested at matched resolution, so this remains an acknowledged limitation [§3.2, p. 12]; and \(A_{\rm Oort}\), being derived from axisymmetric models, cannot capture non-circular/bar/spiral-driven shear or streaming motions that might also drive SFE variation [§3.4, p. 17]. My own observations: the sample explicitly excludes mergers and kinematically disturbed galaxies (Qual = -1, 0) [§2.1, p. 3], so results may not generalize to interacting or starburst systems where shear/pressure dependence of SFE could differ (the authors themselves note this explains a discrepancy with Ellison et al. 2024's larger, merger-inclusive sample, §3.2, p. 11). The CO-to-H2 and Hα-SFR conversion assumptions, while tested for robustness, still carry systematic uncertainty inherent to any single-galaxy calibration applied uniformly.

Assessment

This is a careful, methodologically transparent dynamical/ISM study rather than a machine-learning methods paper — the "ML" here is a modest, well-controlled random forest used purely for relative feature-importance ranking, not a representation-learning or foundation-model application, so it will interest the researcher more for its spectroscopy-derived physical inference (SFR/SFE/stellar population surface densities from full-spectrum fitting and emission-line ratios, tied to JAM dynamical modelling) than for ML novelty. The extensive robustness testing (Table 1, Appendices A-B) is a genuine strength and lends credibility to the shear-SFE ranking, but the authors are commendably honest that the signal is weak (\(R^2=0.437\), \(\rho\approx-0.28\)) and non-universal — this is a nuanced, hedged result rather than a decisive demonstration that shear quenches star formation. The explicit connection to morphological/gravitational quenching and to competing shear-stabilization vs. cloud-collision pictures is conceptually useful for anyone working on quenching mechanisms from spectroscopy. The small sample (29 galaxies) is the main weakness, and the paper itself points to the planned KILOGAS survey (~500 galaxies with DynPop properties already available) as the natural next step [§3.3, p. 16] — worth watching if the researcher tracks MaNGA DynPop-adjacent work.

Code & data

The MaNGA DynPop database and a Python script for mass-distribution calculations are public at https://manga-dynpop.github.io [§2.1, p. 3, footnote]. The KILOGAS survey is referenced at https://kilogas.space/ [§3.3, p. 16, footnote]. No dedicated code repository for the random forest analysis itself is mentioned.

4. Diverse Stellar Components Resolved by JWST in the Massive Galaxy SPT0311-58

Briana Wirag, Jack E. Birkin, Justin S. Spilker, Dazhi Zhou, Manuel Aravena, Melanie Archipley et al. · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF

The authors use new JWST NIRCam+MIRI imaging combined with HST and ALMA photometry to model the SED of the clumpy z=6.9 protocluster galaxy SPT0311-58, deriving a total stellar mass of \(M_\star=3.2^{+3.2}_{-1.6}\times10^{11}\,M_\odot\) and SFR of \(1.5\times10^3\,M_\odot\,{\rm yr}^{-1}\). Comparing to EXCELS quiescent galaxy SFHs, they find SPT0311-58's SFR exceeds what those SFHs predict at this epoch, though it remains a plausible progenitor of massive quiescent galaxies.

Why you might care: Direct comparison of resolved SED-based SFHs to spectroscopically-derived quiescent-galaxy SFHs (EXCELS) is a nice case study in cross-checking photometric stellar population inference against full-spectrum fitting results. (score 5.0 (you 5/10 · field 5/10))

Abstract SPT0311$-$58 is comprised of two clumpy dusty star-forming galaxies inhabiting a $z=6.9$ protocluster core. Within a $16$ kpc diameter, there are 26 spectroscopically confirmed members. The 12 highly dust-obscured clumps were identified by their rest-frame [CII] emission, and the 14 less obscured clumps in the rest-frame ultraviolet (UV). We present new NIRCam and MIRI imaging from JWST of the central regions, which respectively trace the rest-frame UV and near-infrared. Combining this imaging with supplementary data from the Hubble Space Telescope and Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA) provides the most complete picture of SPT0311$-$58 to date. Using 14 photometric bands and ALMA dust continuum to model the spectral energy distribution of SPT0311$-$58, we present well-informed stellar masses and star-formation rates (SFR) on local ($\sim1.5$ kpc) and global scales. We find that SPT0311$-$58 has a total stellar mass of $M_\star=3.2^{+3.2}_{-1.6}\times10^{11}\, M_\odot$ and SFR of $1.5^{+0.89}_{-0.53}\times10^3\, M_\odot\, \text{yr}^{-1}$. We compare these measurements to simulations and known, rare classes of high-redshift objects observed by JWST, namely massive $z\approx6$--$8.5$ Lyman-break galaxies, $z>6$ quasars, and other merging/clumpy $z\sim6$--$7$ systems. SPT0311$-$58 is comparable to $z>6$ quasars in both stellar mass and SFR, but is more extreme than the other reference populations for both quantities. Additionally, we compare our measurements with the star-formation histories (SFH) of five massive quiescent galaxies from the Early eXtragalactic Continuum and Emission Line Science (EXCELS) survey. We find that SPT0311$-$58 has a higher SFR than expected from the SFHs of the EXCELS galaxies at $z=6.9$, but is otherwise a plausible progenitor to massive quiescent galaxies.

5. Non-monotonic Age-metallicity Relations Across the Galactic Disk Revealed by APOGEE Red Clump Stars

Chun Wang, Yang Huang, Haibo Yuan · astro-ph.GA · arXiv · PDF

Using APOGEE red clump stars plus open clusters with precise ages, the authors map the age-metallicity relation (AMR) across Galactocentric radius and height, finding it non-monotonic for ages below ~6-8 Gyr: [Fe/H] dips at intermediate ages then rises again, with an extra reversal and a metallicity peak at ~2 Gyr beyond R~11 kpc. They argue this requires episodic metal-poor gas accretion combined with radial migration, which a toy model qualitatively reproduces.

Why you might care: A clean empirical constraint on chemical-enrichment histories and radial migration that is directly analogous to the SFH/metallicity degeneracies you deal with when fitting galaxy spectra, just resolved star-by-star in the Milky Way. (score 4.2 (you 4/10 · field 5/10))

Abstract Using APOGEE red clump stars and open clusters with precise ages, we investigate the age--metallicity relation (AMR) across the Galactic disk as a function of Galactocentric radius $R$ and vertical height $|Z|$. For stars older than $\sim$6--8 Gyr, the AMR shows little dependence on either $R$ or $|Z|$ within the uncertainties, suggesting rapid and well-mixed chemical evolution in the early disk. In contrast, younger populations exhibit a clear negative radial metallicity gradient, with the inner disk being more metal-rich than the outer disk, consistent with inside-out disk growth since $\sim$6--8 Gyr ago. Most importantly, at fixed location, the AMR for ages $\lesssim6$--8 Gyr is non-monotonic: [Fe/H] decreases toward younger ages, reaches a minimum at intermediate ages, and then rises again. Beyond $R\sim11$\,kpc, the relation becomes even more complex, exhibiting an additional reversal and a distinct metallicity peak at an age of $\sim2$\,Gyr. Such complex behavior cannot be explained by continuous local enrichment alone, but instead points to the combined effects of a multi-phase gas-accretion history and radial migration. Episodes of metal-poor gas inflow can dilute the interstellar medium and reset its chemical evolution, while subsequent enrichment raises the metallicity again; radial migration further reshapes the observed AMR by mixing stellar populations formed at different radii and metallicities. A toy model incorporating inside-out growth, two gas-inflow episodes, and radial migration qualitatively reproduces the observed trends, highlighting the combined roles of episodic gas accretion and radial migration in shaping the chemical evolution of the Galactic disk.

6. Euclid: Quick Data Release (Q1) -- The evolution of the radio-loud AGN fraction-stellar mass relation to \(z\sim3\) in the Euclid Deep Field North

S. Visser (Leiden Observatory, Leiden University, Einsteinweg 55, 2333 CC Leiden, The Netherlands), H. J. A. Rottgering (Leiden Observatory et al. · astro-ph.GA · astro-ph.CO · arXiv · PDF
