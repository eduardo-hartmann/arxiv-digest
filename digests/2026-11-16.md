### 1. Unsupervised learning reveals a small number of galaxy formation pathways in IllustrisTNG

*C. Alvarez-Fernandez, H. Dominguez Sanchez, J. Vega-Ferrero, E. Angeloudi, A. Ferre-Mateu, D. Sotillo-Ramos* · `astro-ph.GA` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17559) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17559)

> **Why it was picked:** Unsupervised ML (UMAP+k-means) on galaxy star-formation histories in simulations directly matches ML-for-galaxy-evolution interests. — score **7.5** (you 8/10 · field 6/10)

#### TL;DR.
The authors apply UMAP and k-means clustering to the star formation histories (SFHs) of ~10000 IllustrisTNG galaxies and find that the diversity of simulated mass-assembly histories collapses into just six characteristic evolutionary pathways that map cleanly onto present-day mass, morphology, kinematics, and scaling relations [Abstract, p. 1].

#### Main question
The paper asks whether a data-driven, fully unsupervised analysis of galaxy star formation histories in a cosmological simulation can reveal a small number of discrete evolutionary pathways, and whether these pathways correlate with present-day observable properties (mass, morphology, kinematics, star formation activity) that were *not* used to define them [§1, p. 2]. This matters because observations give only single-epoch snapshots, so simulations with full merger trees are needed to connect assembly history to the observed diversity of galaxies, and because most prior ML work on TNG/imaging data does not explicitly encode the temporal dimension of formation histories [§1, pp. 1–2].

#### Data & method
The sample combines 6481 TNG100 galaxies ($M_\star>10^{10}\,M_\odot$) and 3167 TNG50 galaxies ($M_\star>10^{9}\,M_\odot$), for 9648 galaxies total, each tracked across 100 snapshots from $z\approx20$ to $z=0$ using the public Stellar Assembly, Mergers, Circularities/Angular Momenta, Morphology, SFR, and Projected Sizes catalogs [§2, pp. 2–4]. Each galaxy's in-situ and ex-situ SFH (100 points each) is concatenated into a 200-dimensional vector, normalized by maximum stellar mass, giving a $9648\times200$ input matrix [§4, p. 5]. UMAP (default parameters: 15 neighbors, min-dist 0.1, Euclidean metric) reduces this to 2D for visualization; PCA and t-SNE were also tried and give qualitatively consistent results (Appendix, Fig. B.1) [§3–4, pp. 4–5]. K-means (compared against hierarchical/Ward clustering, Appendix Fig. C.2) is applied directly to the full 200D vectors, not to the 2D UMAP projection, to avoid discarding structure; $k=6$ is chosen via elbow/silhouette diagnostics (Appendix Fig. C.1), which favor $k=3$–6 with a local silhouette maximum at 6 [§5, pp. 8–9].

#### Main results
1. **UMAP organizes SFH shape into physically ordered regions.** Fig. 3 [p. 6] shows that position in the UMAP plane tracks a quenching gradient (vertical) and an ex-situ-mass gradient (diagonal): steady in-situ SFHs sit at top, fast-quenched low-ex-situ galaxies at bottom-left, major-merger-dominated galaxies at bottom-right.
2. **Properties not used as input still show strong gradients.** Fig. 4 [p. 7] shows smooth gradients in SFR, final stellar mass, $t_{50}$, $t_{90}$ despite SFHs being mass-normalized; Fig. 6 and Fig. 7 [pp. 7–8] show disk/bulge mass fraction, specific angular momentum, and morphological type (PLate, PS0) also vary smoothly across the UMAP plane, even though UMAP was never given this information.
3. **Six clusters capture the main formation channels.** Fig. 8 and Fig. 9 [pp. 9–10], with properties tabulated in Table 1 [p. 11]: C1 (33%, secular star-forming disks, negligible ex-situ), C2 (26%, transition/quenching), C3 (11%, long-quenched intermediate-mass ETGs/S0s), C4 (16%, star-forming with non-negligible ex-situ), C5 (8%, quenched with sustained ex-situ accretion), C6 (6%, least populous, major-merger-dominated, most massive, elliptical, counterintuitively high specific angular momentum).
4. **Clusters occupy distinct, physically sensible loci on scaling relations.** Fig. 12 [p. 13] (SFR–mass plane) shows C1/C4 mostly on the main sequence ($>70\%$), C3/C5 mostly below it ($>80\%$, quantified in Table 2, p. 15); Fig. 13 [p. 14] (mass–size plane) shows C1 following the late-type van der Wel et al. (2014) relation and C3/C5/C6 following the early-type relation, even for C6 where ex-situ mass dominates the SFH.

#### Caveats & limitations
The authors note: the number of clusters is somewhat ambiguous ($k=3$–6 all defensible from elbow/silhouette metrics; $k=5$ merges C5/C6, $k=7$ splits C3 without revealing new pathways) [§5.1, p. 10]; TNG50 and TNG100 have different mass/volume resolution so combined-sample statistics "should be interpreted with caution" (Table 1 note, p. 11); morphology catalogs are TNG100-only at $z=0$ (TNG50 morphology exists only at $z=0.5$) [§2.4, p. 4]; UMAP axes have no intrinsic physical meaning [§4.1, p. 5]; the anti-correlation between ex-situ mass fraction and cold gas fraction could be a normalization artifact rather than a physical effect [§4.1, p. 7]; k-means uses Euclidean distance on SFH vectors, which the authors acknowledge is often suboptimal for time-series data, though they argue normalization mitigates this [§5, p. 9]. My own observations: the paper is purely simulation-based (IllustrisTNG's specific subgrid physics, e.g. feedback prescriptions), so it is unclear how much the six-pathway structure is a property of galaxy formation in general versus an imprint of this particular simulation's physics; the paper does not test against other simulations (e.g., EAGLE, SIMBA) for robustness, and no comparison to observational SFH reconstructions (e.g., from full-spectrum fitting) is attempted, which would be the natural next step for connecting this to real surveys.

#### Assessment
The method itself (UMAP + k-means on SFHs) is methodologically modest and the results — recovering star-forming disks, transition/green-valley galaxies, quenched S0s, massive ellipticals, and merger-driven systems — largely reproduce well-known observational taxonomies, as the authors themselves acknowledge [§5.1, p. 10]. The value added is that these categories emerge from unsupervised clustering of mass-assembly histories alone, without morphology or kinematics as input, which is a reasonably convincing demonstration that a handful of formation channels can explain much of the SFH diversity in this simulation. For this researcher's interests, the paper is most relevant as a complement to spectroscopic SFH recovery: it offers a simulation-based "ground truth" taxonomy of formation pathways that could in principle be compared against SFHs inferred from full-spectrum fitting of DESI/MaNGA/JWST spectra, or used to validate ML-based SFH emulators trained on simulations. However, it does not touch spectroscopy, observational data, or inference uncertainty at all — it is purely a simulation-analysis paper, so its direct transferability to survey spectroscopy work is indirect (a template/benchmark for formation pathways, not a tool applicable to real spectra as-is). The explicit forward pointer to a companion paper using the same framework to identify relic galaxies (Granados-Buey et al., in prep.) [§6, p. 15] suggests the authors see this primarily as infrastructure for targeted simulation-based galaxy-type searches rather than an endpoint.

#### Code & data
Not mentioned (no code repository link given; data come from the public IllustrisTNG catalogs at https://www.tng-project.org/, cited in §2, p. 2).

### 2. Euclid Data Release 1 (DR1) : Performance of the redshift measurement and spectroscopic analysis

*Euclid Collaboration, V. Le Brun (Aix-Marseille Universit\'e, CNRS, CNES, LAM, Marseille et al.* · `astro-ph.CO` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17960) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17960)

> **Why it was picked:** Euclid DR1 flagship spectroscopic pipeline paper on redshift/spectral feature measurement is a major large-survey spectroscopy data release directly relevant to survey spectroscopy interests. — score **7.2** (you 7/10 · field 8/10)

#### TL;DR
This paper documents and validates the pipeline (SPE PF) that produces redshifts, spectral classifications, and emission/absorption-line measurements for Euclid's Data Release 1 (DR1), finding ~98.5% agreement with DESI redshifts in a high-quality visually-vetted sample and good purity for galaxy/star/QSO classification, while flagging persistent "interloper" failure modes at low redshift and for faint continua [Abstract, p. 1].

#### Main question
The paper asks: how good are the redshifts, spectral classifications, and line-flux measurements delivered by Euclid's automated spectroscopic pipeline (SPE PF) in DR1, and where does the pipeline still fail? This matters because Euclid's cosmology goal — measuring galaxy clustering at $0.84<z<1.88$ via Hα — depends on knowing the purity and completeness of the spectroscopic sample, and because the same spectra are a resource for galaxy-evolution science if their reliability can be independently characterized [§1, p. 1].

#### Data & method
The data are NISP slitless near-IR spectra ($R\simeq500$, 1206–1892 nm red grism, RGS; plus blue grism, BGS, in deep fields) for sources brighter than $H_E=23.5$, over 1900 deg$^2$ of the Euclid Wide Survey (EWS) and the three Euclid Deep Fields (EDFs), with EDF-North (EDF-N) having the deepest coverage (seven complete RGS visits, three BGS visits) [§1, p. 1; §4, p. 6]. The SPE PF fits each spectrum on a redshift grid against three model categories — "galaxy" (continuum median-filtered out, only Hα-driven emission-line templates, with an "Hα prior" favoring Hα-plausible solutions), "star" (Pickles 1998 templates plus ultracool dwarf templates), and "qso" (quasar templates plus, newly for DR1, five stacked galaxy templates — quiescent, blue/red star-forming, and type-2 AGN built via spectral stacking of Q1 spectra matched to external redshifts: 12,190 quiescent, 21,641 blue star-forming, 7,810 red star-forming galaxies) — via $\chi^2$ minimization and Bayesian model selection, producing a redshift PDF with up to five ranked solutions [§2.1–2.4, pp. 2–5]. Line fluxes are measured by two independent methods, direct integration (DI) and Gaussian fitting (GF), with a dedicated "QSO mode" deconvolving broad (>1500 km/s) Hα from narrow/host components [§2.3, pp. 3–4]. Validation uses cross-matches to DESI DR1, to deeper Euclid Deep Survey (EDS) spectra, to Subaru FMOS-COSMOS spectra, and a visual-inspection campaign of ~4000 EDF-N sources by volunteer graders over 6–8 weeks [§3–5, pp. 6–11].

#### Main results
1. **Classification purity**: Table 1 [p. 6] shows that, cross-matched against DESI, Euclid's "galaxy" class reaches 98.2% (EWS) / 98.4% (EDS) agreement once restricted to spatially extended sources (removing stellar contamination), stars reach 99.6–99.7% agreement, and QSOs reach 88–95% agreement, though the QSO *category* also absorbs many quiescent galaxies and type-2 AGN by design, and only ~5% of true DESI quasars are recovered as QSOs (most leak into "galaxy") [§3, p. 6].
2. **Redshift accuracy in EDF-N**: Fig. 3 [p. 7] compares EDS and DESI redshifts, showing a precision of $\sigma_{\Delta z/(1+z)}=0.0003$ (vs. 0.0008 in Q1) and no diagonal "line-interloper" tracks (unlike Q1), though noise interlopers remain. The fraction of correct redshifts peaks at 75% in $0.84\le z<1.6$ (for pipeline probability $\ge0.99$) but drops to 42% at $z<0.1$ [§4.1, p. 7]. Combining with photometric redshifts (PHZ) raises this to 98% in the same high-$z$ bin [§4.2, p. 7].
3. **Visual-inspection ground truth**: Of 4000 EDF-N objects graded by eye on a 1–4 scale, Table 2 [p. 8] finds 30% "secure," 51% "reliable," 17% "marginal," and 1.3% "uncertain," corresponding to ~80% of the cosmological target sample having genuinely correct redshifts. Fig. 4 [p. 9] shows median-stacked spectra per quality tier: the Secure/Reliable/Marginal stacks show a coherent, progressively weaker line sequence, while the Uncertain stack shows spurious line-like features from aligned noise, confirming the grading is physically meaningful.
4. **Wide Survey performance and flux validation**: Fig. 5 [p. 11] shows EWS-vs-DESI and EWS-vs-EDS redshift comparisons, revealing a population of $[O\,{\rm III}]$ interlopers absent from Q1 (attributed to an Hα prior tuned on lower-quality Q1 data) and correct-redshift fractions rising from ~10% at $z<0.1$ to 60% at $0.1<z<0.84$ and higher near the Hα window. Fig. 6 [p. 12] validates Hα fluxes: EWS vs. EDS median difference is $-2^{+19}_{-26}\%$, and EWS vs. FMOS is $7^{+43}_{-28}\%$, both consistent with zero within scatter. The DR1 catalogue totals over 160 million EWS redshifts and 7.0 million EDF redshifts (2.7 million in EDF-N) [§6, p. 12].

#### Caveats & limitations
The authors state: classification is biased toward "galaxy" because that model's continuum is a better-fitting median filter rather than a template [§2.2, p. 3]; the Hα prior tuned on Q1 causes new $[O\,{\rm III}]$/other-line interlopers in DR1 [§5.1–5.2, pp. 10–11]; low-redshift ($z<0.1$ or $z<0.84$ without Hα in RGS) performance is poor (<10–60%) [§5.3, p. 10]; stellar templates are external and not yet Euclid-based, degrading faint-star results [§6, p. 12]; and EDF-F/EDF-S have far less visit depth than EDF-N, limiting generalizability of the visual-inspection validation [§4.3, p. 7]. My own observation: the visual-inspection campaign used volunteer, non-blinded graders with variable expertise and only partial multi-grading (51% graded ≥3 times), which could introduce subjective bias not fully quantified [Table 2, p. 8].

#### Assessment
This is a technical/validation paper rather than a science result — it is the methodological backbone that any DR1-based galaxy-evolution or clustering analysis should cite before trusting SPE_PF redshifts or line fluxes. The numbers are concrete and the multi-pronged validation (DESI, EDS depth, FMOS, visual inspection, stacked spectra) is convincing for its stated purpose. For this researcher's interests, the most relevant parts are the stacked-template construction for quiescent/star-forming/AGN classes (§2.4, Fig. 2, p. 5) — a simple but effective data-driven template-building approach relevant to stellar-population work on Euclid spectra — and the ML-based reliability estimator `gal.spe_z_rel`, trained on simulated PDFs (EL-COSMOS) rather than real Euclid data, which is explicitly flagged as suboptimal and a clear target for future ML/representation-learning work on Euclid spectra [§4.3, p. 7]. No new physical results on galaxy evolution are presented; the value here is purely as a reference for data quality when building samples.

#### Code & data
Data: Euclid DR1, archived at the Euclid Science Archive, https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/euclid/data-release-dr1, with detailed data-model documentation at the linked Data Product Description Document [§2.5, p. 4; p. 12]. Code: the `slinefit` line-fitting software used for cross-validation is public at https://github.com/cschreib/slinefit [§5.4, p. 12]. The `SpectraPyle` stacking code is referenced (Euclid Collaboration: Quai et al. 2026) but no repository link is given in this text.

### 3. Counter-rotating Ionized and Neutral Gas in a Star-forming Elliptical Galaxy

*Pralay Biswas, Yogesh Wadadekar, Omkar Bait, Sushma Kurapati* · `astro-ph.GA` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17740) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17740)

> **Why it was picked:** SDSS-MaNGA spectroscopy of gas-star kinematics and stellar population age gradients directly matches survey spectroscopy interests. — score **6.2** (you 7/10 · field 4/10)

**TL;DR.** Combining uGMRT 21 cm imaging with SDSS-MaNGA IFU spectroscopy, the authors find a compact star-forming elliptical, MaNGA 10214-1902, in which a central disk of ionized gas counter-rotates relative to both the stars and a 45 kpc H i disk that itself co-rotates with the stars — a three-component kinematic configuration the authors say has not been reported before in an early-type galaxy [p. 1], [p. 9].

#### Main question
The paper asks where the cold gas fueling star formation in "star-forming ellipticals" (SF-Es) comes from, using kinematic misalignment between gas and stars as a tracer of external gas acquisition [§1, p. 1-2]. This matters because most early-type galaxies (ETGs) are red and gas-poor, yet a minority form stars on the main sequence; a prior MaNGA study of 59 such SF-Es (P. Biswas & Y. Wadadekar 2024) found disturbed kinematics suggestive of recent minor mergers or accretion, but could not directly identify the acquired gas without resolved cold-gas data [§1, p. 1]. The authors note that in known misaligned ETGs (e.g., ATLAS3D), different gas phases are normally kinematically aligned with each other where they overlap, implying one external origin event [§1, p. 2]; galaxies with two distinct misaligned gas components are very rare (fewer than ten known) [§1, p. 2].

#### Data & method
The target, selected from the 59 SF-Es because its MaNGA maps already showed ionized-gas/stellar counter-rotation and its GBT single-dish spectrum showed it was H i-rich [§2.1, p. 2], is a compact ($R_e = 0.78$ kpc) galaxy at $z=0.02178$ (98.1 Mpc) with $\log M_*/M_\odot = 9.90$ [Abstract, p. 1; Tab. 1, p. 4]. New uGMRT Band 5 observations (~9 hr on-source) yield six H i spectral cubes at resolutions from $3.3''\times2.8''$ to $43''\times36''$, reduced with CASA/SoFiA-2 [§2.2, p. 2; Tab. 2, p. 11]. This is combined with SDSS-MaNGA DR17 DAP maps (stellar/ionized-gas kinematics, emission-line fluxes, Dn4000), Pipe3D stellar-population ages, PyMorph morphology, GSWLC-A2/Salim SED-based stellar mass/SFR/dust, and DESI Legacy imaging [§2.2, p. 2-3]. Kinematic position angles (PAs) are measured with fit_kinematic_pa [§3.2, p. 5].

#### Main results
- **H i reservoir and co-rotation with stars:** The galaxy hosts $\log(M_{HI}/M_\odot)=9.65$ (GBT) / $9.55$ (uGMRT) in a 45 kpc H i disk ($\approx60\,R_e$), with $M_{HI}/M_* \approx 0.5$, about 0.5 dex above the xGASS average for its stellar mass; the disk has a central column-density depression (Fig. 1 [p. 3]) and rotates at PA $216^\circ$–$221^\circ$ at every resolution, aligned with the stellar PA within $\Delta PA = 23^\circ\pm20^\circ$ (Table 1 [p. 4]; Appendix B, Fig. 4 [p. 12]).
- **Counter-rotating central ionized gas:** Within the H i depression ($r\lesssim2$ kpc), ionized gas rotates opposite to both the stars ($\Delta PA=152^\circ\pm20^\circ$) and the surrounding H i ($\Delta PA=179^\circ\pm10^\circ$), shown via velocity maps and a major-axis position–velocity diagram where H i/stellar and Hα velocities move in opposite senses along the same slit (Fig. 2 [p. 6]).
- **Star formation, not AGN, powers the center:** [S II] and [O I] BPT diagrams classify 85%/65% of central bins as star-forming (vs. composite/LINER in [N II]-BPT), central EW(Hα)=8.1 Å exceeds the ≲3 Å ceiling for old-star ionization, and the 1.4 GHz radio continuum SFR ($0.21\pm0.03\ M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$) agrees with the SED-based SFR, arguing against a dominant AGN (Fig. 1d [p. 3], Fig. 3 [p. 7], §3.3 [p. 5]).
- **Younger center:** Dn4000 is lowest at the center (1.60 vs 1.67 at 3″–6″) and Pipe3D light-weighted age is younger within $R_e$ (1.9 Gyr) than at larger radii (3.2 Gyr), indicating recent/ongoing central star formation atop an older population (Fig. 3b [p. 7]; §3.4, p. 7).

#### Caveats & limitations
The authors note that H i co-spatial with the ionized gas itself is not detected, so a faint inner H i component with a different (possibly co-rotating or differently warped) orientation cannot be ruled out — meaning the two gas phases are compared at different radii, not literally overlapping [Abstract, p. 1; §3.2, p. 5]. $R_e$ is smaller than the MaNGA PSF, so kinematics within $R_e$ are not fully resolved and radial differences in stellar age are lower limits [§2.2, p. 3; §3.4, p. 7]. The counter-rotation significance of the stellar age gradient is only ≈2σ by formal Pipe3D errors, though corroborated by the model-independent Dn4000 trend [§3.4, p. 7]. Light-fraction estimates are degenerate with metallicity [§3.4, p. 7]. They also note a strong warp could mimic counter-rotation but argue the data disfavor this [§4, p. 8], and that this nested configuration should only survive ~hundreds of Myr, explaining its rarity but also meaning it's a short-lived, possibly lucky catch [§4, p. 8]. My own observation: this is a single-object case study (N=1), so while suggestive for the broader SF-E population, no statistics on how common this exact three-component configuration is are presented.

#### Assessment
This is a well-controlled single-object study cross-checking kinematics across independent datasets (DAP and Pipe3D PAs agree, and multiple H i resolutions give consistent PAs), and it carefully checks for and rules out a dominant AGN, which makes the gas-accretion-fuels-star-formation picture convincing within its stated caveats about the undetected inner H i. For this researcher's interests — spectroscopic diagnostics of stellar populations (Dn4000, light/mass-weighted ages, SFH), gas-phase diagnostics (BPT diagrams), and physical-property inference from spectra+SED — this paper is a nice concrete illustration of how kinematic misalignment plus full-spectrum-derived ages can jointly constrain gas accretion histories in low-mass ETGs, connecting to the quenching/rejuvenation literature (misaligned accretion sustaining main-sequence SFR below $M_*\sim10^{10}M_\odot$, per J. L. Tous et al. 2026 cited in §4 [p. 8]). It's a narrow, descriptive result rather than a methods or large-survey paper, so it's most useful as a motivating case study rather than a tool/technique to adopt directly.

#### Code & data
Not mentioned (no code/data repository link given; software used — CASA, SoFiA-2, astropy, numpy, scipy, fit_kinematic_pa, matplotlib, aplpy — is listed in Acknowledgments [p. 10], but no dataset release or code repository URL is provided).

### 4. Observations of stellar feedback: tracers, diagnostics, measurements, and physical interpretation

*Anna F. McLeod* · `astro-ph.GA` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17592) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17592)

This review frames stellar feedback as an inference problem, surveying how observational diagnostics (ionised gas, bubbles, outflows, Lyman-continuum escape) connect to physical feedback quantities while depending on stellar population synthesis assumptions and environment. The main conclusion is that feedback measurements must be contextualised by mechanism, gas phase, environment, and spatial/temporal scale, and that progress requires matched tracer/scale observations across diverse systems.

**Why you might care:** Directly relevant to stellar population synthesis caveats and systematic uncertainties that also affect full-spectrum fitting inferences of galaxy properties. <small>(score **5.2** (you 5/10 · field 6/10))</small>

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Stellar feedback is central to galaxy evolution, yet the coupling of radiation, stellar winds, and supernovae to the multi-phase interstellar medium remains uncertain. This review treats this as a problem of inference, framed around four qualitative axes of ignorance: observational diagnostics are indirect; stellar population synthesis models must be ground-truthed before being used to infer feedback quantities; recent high-redshift observations have opened a new regime that may require re-calibration; and stellar feedback and the affected environment are interdependent. We examine the inference chain, from observables to physical quantities and from those quantities to how energy and momentum are transferred to the surrounding medium, across the principal diagnostics of stellar feedback: ionised gas, mechanically-driven bubbles, giant molecular cloud lifetimes, galactic outflows, and Lyman-continuum escape. We ask how much observations reveal about the link between the feedback-driving massive stars and the feedback-driven interstellar medium. We further discuss how stellar population synthesis assumptions, environment (e.g., metallicity, dust content), and galaxy properties (e.g., stellar mass, gas content) affect feedback measurements, and how high-redshift observations are testing calibrations established in the nearby Universe. The emerging picture is that interpreting a feedback measurement requires explicit control of the inference chain and of its physical context; feedback should therefore be contextualised, i.e. interpreted in terms of mechanism, gas phase, environment, and spatial and temporal scale.
