### 1. JEPA for astronomy: multi-survey spectral alignment puts stellar parameters on the leading principal axes

*Xiaosheng Zhao, Rosemary F. G. Wyse, Yuan-Sen Ting, Alexander S. Szalay* · `astro-ph.GA` · `astro-ph.IM` · `astro-ph.SR` · `cs.LG` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23492) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.23492)

> **Why it was picked:** JEPA-based multi-survey (Gaia XP, LAMOST, DESI, APOGEE) spectral foundation model with alignment-objective analysis — direct hit on core ML-on-spectra interest. — score **8.5** (you 9/10 · field 7/10)

**TL;DR.** Holding the cross-survey embedding architecture fixed and swapping only the training objective, the authors show that a negative-free, distribution-matching ("JEPA") alignment loss — unlike the standard contrastive (CLIP) alternative — puts effective temperature, surface gravity, [Fe/H] and [α/Fe] on the leading principal-component axes of a shared Gaia XP/LAMOST/DESI/APOGEE embedding, making them linearly readable with no labels used during training [Abstract, p. 1].

#### Main question
The paper asks whether the *alignment objective* used to build multi-survey spectral foundation models determines not just whether stellar physics is encoded, but how easily (linearly) it can be read out — a property they call "legibility" [§1, p. 2]. This matters because most astronomical foundation models borrow CLIP-style contrastive alignment from vision/language, which forces every star apart from every other star in a batch; the authors argue this is an artifact needed only to prevent embedding collapse, not a requirement of stellar physics, and is poorly suited to spectroscopy where many stars are near-identical twins [§2.1, p. 2-3].

#### Data & method
Training data are cross-matched stars between Gaia XP DR3, LAMOST LRS DR11, DESI DR1 and APOGEE DR17, with pair counts from 16,732 (APOGEE–DESI) to 820,568 (Gaia XP–LAMOST) [Tab. 1, p. 4]. Each survey has a frozen, separately pre-trained backbone (autoencoders/transformers) feeding a trainable per-survey head into a shared 768-d space, further mapped by a projector into a 128-d space where the loss acts [Fig. 1, p. 3; Fig. B1, p. 13]. Three objectives are compared at fixed architecture: an unaligned autoencoder (AE, no cross-survey term, "no-alignment floor"), CLIP (contrastive, InfoNCE), and JEPA (RDMReg: an invariance term, Eq. 1, p. 3, plus a sliced-Wasserstein regularizer matching each coordinate's distribution to a rectified Laplace target, Eq. 2, p. 3-4). Each is trained at two-survey (XP+LAMOST) and four-survey scale. Evaluation uses PCA of the Gaia XP embedding plus linear-ridge and nonlinear-MLP probes trained at varying labeled sample sizes, on 113,218 labeled stars (101,891 training / 11,327 withheld) [§2.3, p. 4]. Stars are also assigned to five chemically defined populations (thin disk, thick disk, accreted, very metal-poor, low-α & metal-poor) using APOGEE-based abundance cuts [§3, p. 4-5].

#### Main results
1. **Fig. 2 [p. 6]**: for the four-survey JEPA embedding, PC1–PC2 forms a Kiel-diagram-like plane and PC3 is a clean, ordered gradient in [Fe/H]; CLIP's PC3 gradient is only partially ordered; the unaligned AE shows no ordering at all.
2. **Tab. 2 [p. 7]**: at $N=10{,}000$ labels, ridge regression recovers [α/Fe] with $R^2=0.79$ for four-survey JEPA versus $0.58$ for AE, with CLIP and the two-survey models in between; the advantage grows at $N=200$ (ridge gap of 0.30 in [α/Fe], 0.21 in [Fe/H] versus AE). The gap between nonlinear and linear probe performance (a proxy for how "entangled" the information is) is only 0.05 for the best JEPA model versus 0.16 for AE.
3. **Fig. 3 [p. 6]**: the same PC1–PC2 plane, colored by $\log g$, [α/Fe], and chemical category, shows smooth gravity ordering and clearly separated disk/accreted/metal-poor populations, with median PC3 falling from +5.7 (thin disk) to −22.8 (accreted stars) as median [Fe/H] falls from −0.20 to −1.29. A nonlinear classifier separates thin/thick disk with AUC = 0.83 (linear: 0.55); accreted stars separate from the thick disk with AUC = 0.97.
4. **Fig. 4 [p. 8]**: applying the four-survey JEPA backbone (retrained with regularizer weight $\lambda=10$) to 8.0 million Gaia XP red giants (full phase-space info, $3<R<12$ kpc, $|z|<3$ kpc) reproduces the Milky Way's [α/Fe]–[Fe/H] structure, including the low-/high-α sequences and Gaia–Enceladus/Sausage, from XP spectra alone (resolving power $\sim$50).

#### Caveats & limitations
The authors note: the labeled sample is cross-match/magnitude/color-selected, so population fractions reflect that selection, not the Galaxy's true fractions; extinction (shared across all survey views of a star) is not explicitly removed and may remain entangled with stellar parameters; encoders were pre-trained separately per survey rather than jointly with alignment; the choice to probe the pre-projector head embedding rather than the projected space is not systematically justified; and only *explicit* cross-survey alignment is tested, not implicit/tokenizer-mediated alignment [§6, p. 9]. They also note theoretical identifiability results (von Kügelgen et al., Daunhawer et al., Klindt et al.) don't yet predict *linear* readability or that it should sit on leading axes for this multi-instrument, non-contrastive setting [§6, p. 8]. My own observations: the [Fe/H] axis saturates in the very metal-poor tail (little PC3 change between [Fe/H] ≈ −1.3 and −2.4) [§5, p. 7]; no uncertainties are propagated for the 8-million-star catalog predictions in Fig. 4; and the comparison's batch-size asymmetry (CLIP uses larger batches, 1024/384 vs. JEPA's 256/128) somewhat favors CLIP's reported performance being understated, which the authors acknowledge [App. B, p. 13].

#### Assessment
This is a well-controlled ablation (architecture fixed, only loss varied) that makes a specific, useful methodological point for the field: alignment objective, not alignment per se, governs whether a foundation-model embedding is a usable "coordinate system" or just a black box needing a trained read-out. For someone doing full-spectrum fitting or stellar-population work with large surveys, the appeal is that a negative-free, batch-size-insensitive objective scales naturally as more surveys are added (§6, p. 9) — directly relevant to combining DESI/SDSS/4MOST/WEAVE-style heterogeneous spectroscopy without needing huge contrastive batches. The Kiel-diagram re-discovery (Fig. 2) is a nice unsupervised sanity check, though the central evidence is empirical/correlational (R² tables) rather than a theoretical guarantee of legibility, which the authors themselves flag as unresolved [§6, p. 8]. Most directly extends work in the Zhao/Ting SpecCLIP lineage listed among the paper's own citations.

#### Code & data
Observational data are from public archives (Gaia XP, LAMOST, DESI, APOGEE); the code builds on the public SpecCLIP repository (https://github.com/Xiaosheng-Zhao/SpecCLIP), whose Gaia XP/LAMOST encoders and weights are already public, and the authors state the full code and trained weights of this work will be released upon acceptance [Data Availability, p. 11].

### 2. The Gas-Phase Metallicity and Nitrogen abundances in typical galaxies at $z\sim5$

*Claudia Di Cesare, Gauri Kotiwale, Jorryt Matthee, Alice Shapley, Alba Covelo-Paz, Kasper E. Heintz et al.* · `astro-ph.GA` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24630) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.24630) · [💻 code/data](https://github.com/spacetelescope/jwst) · [💻 code/data](https://github.com/HarleyKatz/)

> **Why it was picked:** JWST grism-stacked gas-phase metallicity/MZR study of z~5 star-forming galaxies — galaxy spectroscopy and chemical enrichment, solidly in core topics. — score **6.0** (you 6/10 · field 6/10)

**TL;DR.** Using stacked JWST/NIRCam grism spectra of 1748 Hα-selected galaxies at $z=3.8-6.6$ (⟨z⟩=4.6), the authors measure the mass–metallicity relation (MZR) out to unusually high stellar masses ($\log(M_\star/M_\odot)\sim10.2$) via [Nii]/Hα and [Sii]/Hα, finding a steeper-than-some-previous-studies slope, metallicities below the local Fundamental Metallicity Relation (FMR), and no evidence for nitrogen enhancement in the typical (stacked) high-$z$ population [p. 1].

#### Main question
The paper asks how the gas-phase mass–metallicity relation and the nitrogen-to-oxygen (N/O) abundance ratio behave in *typical*, representative star-forming galaxies at $z\sim5$, across a wide stellar-mass range, rather than in the individually bright, line-detected galaxies that dominate current JWST/NIRSpec samples [§1, p. 2]. This matters because prior MZR measurements disagree on slope evolution at $z>3$ (some find flattening, others steepening) [p. 2], and because selection on individual emission-line detections can bias inferred metallicity distributions [p. 2]. The authors also ask whether the super-solar N/O ratios reported in some individual high-$z$ galaxies hold for the typical population [§1, p. 3].

#### Data & method
The sample combines Hα emitters from five deep NIRCam wide-field slitless spectroscopy (WFSS) surveys — ALT, COLA1, CONGRESS, EIGER and FRESCO — covering $\approx320\,\mathrm{arcmin}^2$ and $>200$ hours of JWST time [§2.1, p. 3]. After quality cuts (SNR(Hα)$>5$, $\chi^2_\mathrm{red}<5$, visual inspection), the final sample is $N=1748$ galaxies at $z=3.8-6.6$ [§2.3, p. 4]. Stellar masses and SFRs come from Bagpipes SED fitting (Bruzual & Charlot models, non-parametric "bursty continuity" SFH, Calzetti dust law) [§3.1, p. 4]; SFRs on $\sim10$ Myr timescales use a new Hα-SFR calibration from SPHINX simulations (Kramarenko et al. 2025) [Eq. 1, p. 6]. Because individual [Nii]/[Sii] detections are rare and biased toward massive, bright sources (only $\sim6\%$ and $\sim1\%$ of the sample respectively, p. 4), the core method is to stack 2D emission-line spectra in 0.5 dex stellar-mass bins and fit Voigt profiles to Hα, [Nii]λ6584/6549 and the resolved [Sii]λλ6716,6731 doublet (resolution $R\sim1600$) [§3.3, p. 6–7]. Metallicities use new strong-line calibrations built from high-redshift auroral-line samples (Sanders et al. 2026), chosen because local calibrations may not capture high-$z$ ionization conditions [§3.5, p. 7–8]. N/O is inferred from the N2S2 ratio via a high-$z$ calibration from Cataldi et al. (2026a) [Eq. 2, p. 8].

#### Main results
1. **MZR shape and normalization**: the [Nii]-based MZR has slope $\gamma=0.42^{+0.10}_{-0.07}$ and normalization $12+\log(\mathrm{O/H})=7.94^{+0.06}_{-0.09}$ at $\log(M_\star/M_\odot)=9$ [Eq. 3, Tab. 2, p. 9]; oxygen abundance spans $12+\log(\mathrm{O/H})\approx7.6-8.4$ (i.e. $Z\approx0.1-0.5\,Z_\odot$) [Fig. 5, p. 9]. The [Sii]-based relation gives a consistent slope ($\gamma=0.35^{+0.13}_{-0.10}$) but $\sim0.3$ dex higher normalization [Tab. 2, p. 9]. This extends stacked high-$z$ MZR constraints to $\sim1$ dex higher stellar mass than prior studies [p. 1].
2. **Comparison to simulations**: at the high-mass end, where models diverge most, the data track the upper envelope defined by COLIBRE and FLARES — both of which include AGN feedback — while SPHINX and dustyGadget (no AGN feedback) predict metallicities up to $0.3-0.5$ dex lower, suggesting AGN feedback may help set chemical enrichment in massive high-$z$ galaxies [§4.2, Fig. 5, p. 11–12].
3. **Electron density**: resolving the [Sii] doublet in stacks yields a median $n_e=184^{+226}_{-169}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$, consistent with the low-density limit and with other stacked (though not individual) high-$z$ measurements [§3.4, p. 7].
4. **N/O ratio**: combining [Nii] and [Sii], the stacked N/O–O/H relation follows the classical primary/secondary nitrogen production trend seen locally, with no evidence of the nitrogen enhancement reported in some individual UV-line-emitting high-$z$ galaxies [Fig. 6, p. 9; §4.3, p. 12–13].
5. **FMR offset**: the sample lies systematically below the local FMR, by $0.10-0.21$ dex (mean $0.15$ dex) over the mass/SFR range constrained by SDSS, and by $0.25-0.28$ dex in the lowest-mass bins [Fig. 8, p. 14; §5.2, p. 14–15].

#### Caveats & limitations
The authors note: below the stellar-mass completeness limit ($\log(M_\star/M_\odot)\lesssim8.5$), their Hα-flux-limited stacks sit $\sim0.6$ dex above the star-forming main sequence, biasing those bins toward high sSFR and, if an FMR holds, toward underestimated metallicity [§3.2, p. 6–7; §5.1, p. 14]. [Nii] and [Sii] become intrinsically faint and poorly calibrated below $12+\log(\mathrm{O/H})\lesssim7.7$ [§5.1, p. 14]. N2-based metallicities can be biased by evolving N/O, and S2 by uncertain S/O evolution with redshift [§3.5, p. 7–8; §5.1, p. 14]. Literature MZR comparisons are complicated by differing diagnostics, calibrations and selection functions [§5.1, p. 13]. My own observation: the paper relies on a single strong-line calibration set (Sanders et al. 2026) as fiducial, and while Appendix B compares alternatives, the quoted headline numbers are calibration-dependent by design — a reader should treat the $\sim0.3$ dex N2/S2 normalization gap as a systematic floor, not statistical uncertainty.

#### Assessment
This is a solid, large-sample observational study whose main value is sample size and a clean, flux-limited selection function rather than a new physical mechanism — it directly tests (and partly validates) earlier claims from more selection-biased NIRSpec studies, and extends the MZR to higher mass than prior stacked work [p. 1, p. 11]. The FMR offset result is a useful independent corroboration since it isn't built on individually-detected auroral lines. For readers working on full-spectrum fitting and SFHs, the explicit SED-fitting caveats (SFH-dependent mass biases, dust-curve choice) are worth noting [§3.1, p. 4–5]. The comparison to simulations with/without AGN feedback (COLIBRE, FLARES vs. SPHINX, dustyGadget) is a nice, if still circumstantial, structural constraint [p. 11–12]. Given the stacking approach, this connects well to methods for inferring population-average physical properties under heavy selection effects — relevant to any work combining survey selection functions with spectral stacking or fitting.

#### Code & data
Not mentioned for the authors' own reduction/fitting pipeline. Public tools used are cited (Bagpipes, pyneb, lmfit, grizli); simulation data access is given for SPHINX20 and COLIBRE in footnotes to Appendix D [p. 19–20], and the DESI DR1 value-added catalog URL is given in a footnote [p. 9].

### 3. Stars and gas in the most metal-poor galaxies -- II. Search for VMS and determination of stellar population and nebular properties at $Z\lesssim0.1\,Z_\odot$

*Aida Wofford, Oskar Arangur\'e, Christophe Morisset, Calum Hawcroft, Claus Leitherer, Svea Hern\'andez et al.* · `astro-ph.GA` · `astro-ph.SR` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23689) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.23689)

> **Why it was picked:** Stellar population synthesis modeling (STARBURST99/GALAXEV/BPASS) of metal-poor galaxy spectra, core topic though single-object focused. — score **5.5** (you 6/10 · field 4/10)

**TL;DR.** The authors obtain new HST/COS UV spectra of eight nearby extremely metal-poor galaxies ($Z\lesssim0.1\,Z_\odot$) selected as candidate Very Massive Star (VMS) hosts via their narrow nebular He II $\lambda4686$ emission, and find no direct or indirect evidence for VMS in any of them, while uncovering that even combined stellar+nebular diagnostics require unrealistic model parameters to reproduce the most extreme line ratios [Abstract, p. 1].

#### Main question
The paper asks whether narrow nebular He II emission in extremely metal-poor galaxies (XMPGs, $Z\lesssim0.1\,Z_\odot$) traces the presence of Very Massive Stars ($>100\,M_\odot$), and more broadly what the UV+optical spectra imply about the ages/masses of the OB-star populations and the dominant ionizing sources in these systems [§1, p. 3]. This matters because VMS are thought to shape stellar black-hole formation, chemical enrichment, and possibly reionization-era galaxies, yet only about twenty are confirmed in the Milky Way/LMC and none at these low metallicities [§1, pp. 2–3].

#### Data & method
The sample is eight nearby ($z\approx0.003$–$0.013$) star-forming galaxies, seven XMPGs plus one sub-SMC-metallicity galaxy, chosen for narrow He II $\lambda4686$ and the absence of classical Wolf-Rayet "blue/red bump" features in archival SDSS spectra [Tab. 1, p. 4; Fig. 2, p. 4]. New HST/COS G140L UV spectra (program 15220, PI Wofford) cover $\approx1121$–$2148$ Å [§2.2–2.3, p. 3]. The galaxies split into "group 1" (three galaxies with P-Cygni-like N V 1240/C IV 1550 stellar-wind profiles: J1016, J1230, J2230) and "group 2" (nebular-dominated C IV, higher EW(H$\beta$)) [§3, p. 6]. The authors compare three independent spectral-synthesis codes — STARBURST99/pySTARBURST99, GALAXEV (CB19), and BPASS v2.2 — run with and without VMS (IMF upper limits of 100 vs. 300 $M_\odot$) at $Z=0.001$–$0.004$ and ages 1–9 Myr [Tab. 4, p. 7; Appendix A, pp. 20–22]. For group 1, they fit these models directly to stellar wind lines (P V, N V, Si IV, C IV) to derive ages, UV continuum slopes $\beta$, dust reddening, and stellar masses [§5, pp. 11–15]. For all galaxies, they feed the same stellar ionizing spectra into Cloudy photoionization grids (pySTARBURST99-based, Aranguré et al. in prep.; and updated CB19-based, Martínez-Paredes et al. 2023) and compare predicted optical (BPT) and UV (C IV/He II, C III]/He II, Si III]/He II) line ratios against the observations [§6, pp. 15–18; Tab. 10, p. 18].

#### Main results
1. Stellar-line fitting gives light-weighted ages for J1016, J1230, J2230 that are all $>3$ Myr in every model/metallicity combination tried (Tab. 5, p. 13; e.g. averaged ages of 5.1, 4.0, and 3.6 Myr at $Z=0.001$, Tab. 7, p. 15) — too old for VMS, whose lifetimes are $<3$ Myr, so no direct VMS signature is found [§5.1–5.2, pp. 11, 15].
2. Model intercomparison (Fig. 8, p. 10) shows that at 2 Myr the classical He II $\lambda1640$ emission expected from VMS is absent or very weak in the GALAXEV CB19-300 and weak in the BPASS sin-300/bin-300 models, meaning current VMS-inclusive codes don't even produce a clean stellar VMS signpost at this metallicity — a caveat that limits any search based on stellar lines alone [§4.1, p. 8–9].
3. In BPT and UV diagnostic diagrams (Figs. 14–15, pp. 17–18), models of photoionization by massive stars without Lyman-continuum (LyC) escape cannot reach the extreme line ratios of the two most metal-poor galaxies (J0944, J1044) or the reference galaxy SBS0335-052E, even when VMS are included; only models with LyC escape (or unrealistically low N/O or high ionization parameter) approach these points [§6.3.1–6.3.2, pp. 16–18].
4. No indirect (nebular) evidence for VMS is found in the group-2 galaxies either, since their derived ages exceed the VMS lifetime and even LyC-escape models mostly fail to reproduce observed He II/H$\beta$ ratios at those ages, except for J1230 [§6.3.1, p. 18; §7, p. 19].

#### Caveats & limitations
The authors note: the sample is small (eight galaxies, two of which — J1119, J1132 — had to be excluded from UV diagnostic diagrams due to noisy/ambiguous He II 1640 detections, Appendix B, p. 23); spectral synthesis models in the XMP regime are poorly constrained and the three codes disagree on best-fit ages by up to 4.5 Myr for the same galaxy (Tab. 6, p. 14); formal $\chi^2_r$ uncertainties are likely underestimated due to correlated errors from resampling/rectification (§5.1, p. 11); and reproducing observed line ratios can require "unrealistic" N/O, ionization parameter, or LyC escape values (§6.3, Abstract, p. 1). They attribute the VMS non-detection to possible rarity (IMF shape, short lifetimes), limited sample size, or model shortcomings, including that the Gräfener & Vink (2015) narrow-line VMS model is not yet implemented in any spectral synthesis code [§7, p. 20]. An additional limitation not emphasized by the authors: the derived ages/masses depend on manually-placed continuum nodes and on assumed covering fractions for Ly$\alpha$ correction (§2.4, p. 5), introducing modeling choices that are hard to independently verify.

#### Assessment
This is a careful, data-driven null result rather than a methods paper: the value lies in the systematic three-code comparison (STARBURST99/GALAXEV/BPASS) in a metallicity regime where such cross-checks barely existed, and in demonstrating concretely that current VMS-inclusive models don't yet give a clean He II 1640 stellar signature at $Z\lesssim0.1\,Z_\odot$. For stellar-population-synthesis-focused readers, the honest reporting of model disagreement (Tab. 5, 6) and the explicit caveat that even with VMS and LyC escape, models can't reproduce the most extreme line ratios without unphysical parameters, is the most transferable takeaway — it's a cautionary case study in how model systematics, not just statistics, limit full-spectrum-fitting inferences at low metallicity. It connects to full-spectrum fitting and SFH/age inference more broadly (the age/reddening/mass degeneracy via the $\beta$-slope, §4.2) and to debates about multiple simultaneous ionizing sources in extreme emission-line galaxies, relevant to interpreting JWST-era high-$z$ analogs. It is not an ML paper and has no direct bearing on data-driven/representation-learning methods.

#### Code & data
HST/COS data are available in MAST under program ID 15220 [Data Availability, p. 21]. No analysis code or model grids are stated to be publicly released in the text.

### 4. Searching the Pareto Front: Multi-Objective Retrieval for Heterogeneous Scientific Modalities

*Song Young Oh, Nesar Ramachandra, Bin Xia, Ian Foster, Kyle Chard* · `cs.AI` · `astro-ph.IM` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23639) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.23639) · _cross_

The paper asks whether late-fused, independently trained modality embeddings can rival multimodal foundation models for cross-modal galaxy retrieval across star-formation histories, spectra, and multi-wavelength imaging. Using Pareto-optimal multi-objective fusion instead of a shared embedding space, the method preserves modality-specific distance structure and yields nearest neighbours 8% closer to the query in raw-data space than a foundation-model kNN baseline (spread 0.128 vs 0.139).

**Why you might care:** Directly relevant to building/evaluating multimodal retrieval over spectra and SFHs, offering an alternative to CLIP-style foundation models with better interpretability of which physical signal drives similarity. <small>(score **5.5** (you 6/10 · field 4/10))</small>

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

In cosmology, surveys span different sky regions and wavelength ranges, resulting in uneven modality coverage across observational datasets. Such heterogeneity can be addressed through either multimodal foundation models (FMs), which learn shared embedding spaces, or independently learned modality embeddings combined via late fusion. We investigate whether the latter provides an adaptive alternative to FM-based scientific retrieval. We evaluate this approach on a galaxy retrieval task spanning diverse modalities--history of star formation, spectra of light emitted, and multi-wavelength observations from two telescopes--comparing an FM baseline with separately trained embeddings fused through multi-objective optimization based on Pareto optimality. We find that the FM baseline achieves strong embedding-space proximity, but our method preserves modality-specific distance structure that allows scientists to inspect which physical signals drive similarity.
