### 1. The LAMOST Galaxy Parameter Catalog (LAGC): Stellar Populations, Kinematics, and Emission Lines of Galaxies in the LAMOST Spectroscopic Survey

*Jia-Feng Lu, Shi-Yin Shen, Qian Liu, Shuai Feng, Yong Shi* · `astro-ph.GA` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20917) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20917)

> **Why it was picked:** LAMOST Galaxy Parameter Catalog delivers full-spectrum-fitting stellar populations, kinematics and emission lines for a large galaxy spectroscopic survey, squarely matching core interests. — score **7.5** (you 8/10 · field 6/10)

**TL;DR.** The authors present LAGC, a homogeneous value-added catalogue of stellar population, kinematic, and emission-line measurements for 283,691 LAMOST galaxy spectra, produced by a new automated pipeline (LAG2P) that solves LAMOST's flux-calibration and variable line-spread-function problems simultaneously [p. 1].

#### Main question
Can a single automated pipeline extract reliable stellar populations, stellar/gas kinematics, and emission-line strengths simultaneously from LAMOST galaxy spectra, given that LAMOST spectra are not absolutely flux calibrated and have a line spread function (LSF) that varies with wavelength, fibre, and time [p. 1, p. 3]? This matters because, despite LAMOST's large galaxy sample complementing SDSS in the northern sky, prior work had only extracted one quantity at a time (stellar populations, kinematics, or emission lines separately), with no joint, self-consistent, continuously updated catalogue [§1, p. 3].

#### Data & method
The pilot release processes the full galaxy-classified sample from LAMOST DR13 (October 2011–May 2025, 6,961 plates, 13.5 million low-resolution spectra), yielding 283,691 galaxy spectra (227,239 unique objects, 41,158 with repeat observations) [§2.5, p. 11-12]. The pipeline (LAG2P) fits 144 E-MILES SSP templates (Padova isochrones, Salpeter IMF; ages 0.06–13 Gyr, [M/H] −1.71 to +0.22) convolved with a wavelength-dependent LSF, plus 19 Gaussian emission-line templates in three kinematic components (stellar, Balmer, forbidden) [§2.1, p. 4-6; Tab. 1, p. 6]. The LSF is derived from a machine-learning instrumental-profile model (Liu et al. 2026) trained on LAMOST arc-lamp exposures [§2.2, p. 7]. The core innovation is the SEW method (Lu et al. 2025): fitting is done in equivalent-width space, EW(λ)=F(λ)/F′(λ) with F′ from Discrete Penalised Least Squares smoothing, which cancels multiplicative flux-calibration/dust distortions [Eq. 3-7, p. 8]; fitting is linearised and solved via NNLS, implemented in the public PPXF-SEW package [§2.3, p. 9]. Uncertainties come from an adaptive Δχ² scan plus hundreds of bootstrap realisations per spectrum [§2.4, p. 10].

#### Main results
1. **Internal reproducibility**: repeat LAMOST observations of the same galaxies show tight one-to-one agreement in age, metallicity, M/L, σ⋆, σgas, and EW(Hα), with median deviations consistent with zero at all SNR [Fig. 3-4, p. 13-14]. Normalised residuals (using bootstrap errors) follow a standard normal distribution with σ≈1, validating the uncertainty estimates [Fig. 5, p. 15].
2. **External cross-check with SDSS**: for 125,388 matched galaxies, offsets are small — Δlog Age≈−0.08 dex, Δ[M/H]≈−0.08 dex, Δlog σ⋆≈−0.02 dex (~−4%), with scatters ~0.21, 0.21, 0.11 dex respectively, converging toward zero at high SNR; gas velocity dispersion shows a systematic offset, σ²gas,SDSS≈σ²gas,LAMOST+(50 km/s)², attributed to aperture and LSF-modelling differences since SDSS lacks a full LSF characterisation [Fig. 6-7, p. 16-17].
3. **Science demonstrations reproduce known relations**: the mass–metallicity relation for old stellar populations [Fig. 8-9, p. 18-19]; a bimodal Hα EW distribution with a valley at ~3 Å separating quiescent (peak ~0.9 Å) from star-forming (peak ~20 Å) galaxies, consistent with the HII-region/diffuse-ionised-gas dichotomy, and BPT diagrams built from EW ratios reproducing the standard star-forming sequence [Fig. 10, Tab. 3, p. 20-21]. Light/mass-weighted age and metallicity differences versus mass are interpreted as evidence for downsizing and gas-inflow dilution in star-forming galaxies [p. 18-19].
4. **Kinematics**: stellar, Balmer- and forbidden-line velocity dispersions follow nearly identical σ–M⋆ relations for star-forming galaxies (slopes ~0.36–0.38, Tab. 4, p. 23), while quiescent galaxies show a shallower slope (0.30) matching Zahid et al. (2016)'s SDSS relation (0.29) [Fig. 11, p. 23].

#### Caveats & limitations
Authors note: the fiducial template set (fixed Salpeter IMF, solar abundances, no α-enhancement/TP-AGB) will be expanded in future releases [§2.1, p. 6]; ~0.6% of spectra fail (mostly negative-flux background-subtraction errors) [§2.5, p. 11]; EW measurements below ~1 Å are unreliable, possibly noise-driven overfits rather than real DIG signal [§4.2, p. 20]; quiescent-galaxy gas kinematics are too noisy to interpret physically [§4.3, p. 21-22]; velocity dispersions are uncorrected fibre-aperture values and may be affected by aperture effects, and low-mass σ measurements approach the instrumental resolution limit [§4.3, p. 22]; a single LOS velocity distribution and Gaussian emission profiles are assumed, so AGN or kinematically decoupled systems need case-by-case treatment [§2.5, p. 11]. My own observation: the inflow-dilution interpretation of the growing Δ[M/H] with mass is explicitly offered as "a plausible interpretation" rather than demonstrated, and the paper does not quantify how much of this trend could stem from age–metallicity degeneracy or TP-AGB modelling, which the authors also flag as an open systematic [p. 19].

#### Assessment
This is a solid, methodologically careful data-product paper rather than a discovery paper — its contribution is a validated pipeline and catalogue, with the "science demonstrations" (§4) serving mainly as validation that known scaling relations are recovered, not new results. The SEW equivalent-width-space trick for calibration-insensitive full-spectrum fitting [§2.3, p. 8] is a genuinely useful idea for the many fibre-fed surveys with imperfect flux calibration, and the combination with an ML-based LSF model (Liu et al. 2026) is a nice practical synthesis directly relevant to upcoming massive surveys (4MOST, WEAVE, PFS) facing similar calibration/LSF headaches — squarely in the researcher's interest in large-survey spectroscopy and stellar population synthesis pipelines. The bootstrap validation via repeat observations [Fig. 5, p. 15] is a convincing, underused quality-control technique that could be valuable to borrow for other survey pipelines. It is not an ML/representation-learning paper in the deep-learning sense — the "machine learning" component is narrowly an LSF emulator (Liu et al. 2026), not the catalogue pipeline itself — so it's of secondary rather than core interest relative to the researcher's ML-for-spectroscopy focus, but highly relevant as a reference pipeline/catalogue for anyone doing stellar-population statistics with LAMOST data.

#### Code & data
Catalogue: China-VO/NADC data platform, https://nadc.china-vo.org/res/r101899/ [§2.5, p. 11]. Fitting code: PPXF-SEW, described as a public Python package (Lu et al. 2025), extending PPXF [§2.3, p. 9]. No separate repository link for LAG2P itself is given in the text.

### 2. Model-Driven Stellar Spectroscopy with the Total Differential Spectral Model (TDSM): Framework and Its Application to 11 Million LAMOST Low-Resolution Spectra

*Lanya Mou, Maosheng Xiang, Haibo Yuan, Tianmin Wu, Meng Zhang* · `astro-ph.GA` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20633) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20633)

> **Why it was picked:** Neural-network emulator for stellar spectra applied to millions of LAMOST spectra directly matches emulator/ML-on-spectra and large-survey interests. — score **6.8** (you 7/10 · field 6/10)

**TL;DR.** The authors introduce TDSM, a neural-network emulator that is trained to reproduce not just synthetic stellar spectra but also their analytic first-derivatives with respect to each stellar parameter, and apply it to derive eight stellar labels for 11 million LAMOST DR10 low-resolution spectra spanning $2500$–$45{,}000$ K, far wider than prior data-driven pipelines [p. 1].

#### Main question
The paper asks how to build a model-driven (as opposed to data-driven) spectral emulator that is accurate across the full, high-dimensional parameter space of a low-resolution survey like LAMOST (R≃1800–2000), including hot, cool, metal-poor and chemically peculiar stars that data-driven methods under-sample or exclude [§1, p. 1–2]. This matters because data-driven approaches (trained only on survey stars with external reference labels) are internally precise but limited in parameter coverage and dimensionality, while existing model-driven (grid-interpolation or Payne-style) pipelines for low-resolution spectra have so far been restricted to narrower temperature/abundance ranges [§1, p. 2].

#### Data & method
The core idea, generalizing earlier "Payne"-style emulators, is to train the neural network simultaneously on ab initio flux $f(\lambda;\vec\theta_{ref})$ and its first-order partial derivatives $\nabla_{\vec\theta}f$ with respect to each parameter, computed directly from the Kurucz/ATLAS12/SYNTHE model atmospheres rather than learned implicitly [Eq. 1–2, §2.1, p. 2–4]. The training library has 14,901 Kurucz synthetic spectra sampled from MIST isochrones in $T_{\rm eff}$, $\log g$, [Fe/H] plus uniformly sampled $v_{\rm micro}$, [α/Fe], [C/Fe], [N/Fe] [Tab. 1, Fig. 1, p. 3], with differential spectra computed for 689 reference points [§2.2, p. 3–4]. The network is a small fully-connected architecture (two hidden layers, 128/256 neurons) trained with a combined flux+gradient loss [Eq. 3, §2.3, p. 4], split into six wavelength segments. Parameters are fit to observed, rest-framed, locally-normalized LAMOST spectra (two Gaussian-kernel widths, 25 Å and 100 Å, for atomic- vs. molecular-band-dominated stars) using Levenberg–Marquardt curve fitting, with heuristics to handle initial-value sensitivity near A0-type stars and an artificial "A-star desert" [§3.1–3.2.3, p. 6–10]. Final parameters are externally calibrated against APOGEE DR17, GALAH DR3, PASTEL, Li et al. (2022), IRFM $T_{\rm eff}$, and DD-Payne $\log g$ [§4, p. 10–13].

#### Main results
(1) Self-validation on Kurucz spectra: pixel-wise flux residual scatter of $5.3\times10^{-4}$ and gradient residual scatter of $3.8\times10^{-5}$ (over an order of magnitude smaller), shown in Fig. 2 [p. 5]. (2) Parameter recovery on noise-added synthetic test spectra (S/N=50) gives scatters of $\sim$36 K ($T_{\rm eff}$), 0.16 dex ($\log g$; 0.11 dwarfs/0.23 giants), 0.06 dex ([Fe/H]), 0.04 dex ([α/Fe]), 0.05 dex ([C/Fe]), 0.20 dex ([N/Fe]), shown in Fig. 5 [p. 7]. (3) Applied to 11,473,640 LAMOST DR10 stellar spectra (8 million unique stars), the catalog delivers $T_{\rm eff}$, $\log g$, $v_{\rm micro}$, [Fe/H] (LTE and NLTE), [α/Fe], [C/Fe], [N/Fe], [Mg/Fe], with repeat-observation internal precision at S/N=100 reaching $\sim$30 K, 0.08 dex, 0.04 dex, 0.02 dex, 0.03 dex, 0.05 dex respectively for metal-rich FGK stars (Fig. 9, p. 14). External comparison to high-resolution surveys gives scatters of 0.088 dex (NLTE [Fe/H] vs. GALAH), 0.071–0.102 dex (LTE [Fe/H]), and 0.050/0.084/0.138 dex for [Mg/Fe]/[C/Fe]/[N/Fe] vs. APOGEE (Fig. 10, p. 16). (4) Coverage: the resulting Kiel and [Fe/H]–$T_{\rm eff}$ diagrams (Fig. 14, p. 19) extend smoothly from 2500 K to 45,000 K and down to [Fe/H] $\approx -4$, with 155,638 metal-poor ([Fe/H]<−1) and 27,321 very metal-poor ([Fe/H]<−2) stars identified (Fig. 16, p. 20) — notably broader than existing LAMOST catalogs (LASP, DD-Payne), which show hard cuts or suppressed densities for hot stars, low metallicities, and bright giants (Fig. 17, p. 21).

#### Caveats & limitations
The authors state that systematic errors from ab initio model imperfections are "significant and complex" and only partly removed by calibration, especially for $\log g$ [§6, Discussion, p. 21]; LTE assumptions make parameters for $T_{\rm eff}\gtrsim7000$ K suspect, pending future NLTE modeling [p. 21–22]; cool star ($T_{\rm eff}\lesssim4000$–4500 K) abundances are flagged as poorly calibrated due to molecular-opacity modeling limitations, and an unexplained bifurcation appears in the M-dwarf/M-giant Kiel diagram [§5.1, p. 17]; an artifact "A-star desert" and associated vertical stripes near $T_{\rm eff}\approx10{,}000$ K persist from the data-combination scheme [§5.1, p. 17]; and the noise model used for synthetic validation may conflate instrumental residuals with Kurucz model systematics, making reported uncertainties conservative (Appendix A, p. 23). The paper does not provide an estimate of how its 11,000/3,901 train/validation split, or the sparse 689-point gradient grid, might limit accuracy in under-sampled corners of parameter space (my own observation, not discussed in text).

#### Assessment
The gradient-matching loss is a genuinely interesting, well-motivated twist on Payne-style emulation — forcing the network to match physically meaningful derivatives rather than just interpolate flux is a sensible way to curb the known problem of implicit, noisy gradient learning in low-resolution, blended spectra [§2.1, p. 2–3]. The validation is thorough (self-validation, synthetic noise injection, repeat observations, multiple external high-resolution anchors) and the resulting catalog's much broader coverage in $T_{\rm eff}$/[Fe/H]/evolutionary-phase space than existing LAMOST products is a concrete, useful deliverable for Galactic archaeology and stellar population work. For this researcher's interests, the paper is adjacent rather than central: it is firmly stellar (not galaxy) spectroscopy, but the methodological pattern — physically-informed neural emulators trained jointly on forward-model outputs and their derivatives, then calibrated against external references — is a transferable idea for full-spectrum galaxy fitting/emulation and for thinking about systematic floors in ML-derived physical parameters from large surveys (DESI/SDSS/4MOST-scale). The companion paper on distances, masses, ages, and binarity (in preparation) [§6, p. 22] would likely be of more direct relevance.

#### Code & data
"The stellar parameter catalog is made publicly available" [§7, p. 22], but no specific repository, URL, or software name/link for the catalog or the TDSM/Curvefit code is given in the text beyond naming the Curvefit Python package used for fitting [§2.4, p. 4] and the general LAMOST DR10 data portal (http://www.lamost.org/dr10/) [p. 5].

### 3. Gravitational Potential of CALIFA Galaxies from the Blue Cloud to the Red Sequence

*Veselina Kalinova* · `astro-ph.GA` · `astro-ph.CO` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20940) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.20940)

> **Why it was picked:** CALIFA IFU spectroscopic survey study of circular velocity vs. quenching (blue cloud to red sequence) connects to galaxy evolution/quenching from spectroscopy. — score **5.5** (you 6/10 · field 4/10)

**TL;DR.** Using 238 CALIFA galaxies with existing dynamical (circular velocity curve) and stellar-population measurements, this proceedings paper shows that both the peak and asymptotic circular velocity rise smoothly and roughly linearly as galaxies move from the star-forming main sequence down through the Green Valley to the Red Sequence, with the inner-potential tracer ($V_{c,\max}$) responding more steeply to this quenching offset than the outer/halo tracer ($V_{c,\mathrm{flat}}$) [p. 1].

#### Main question
The paper asks how the gravitational potential (traced by circular velocity curves, CVCs) relates to star-formation quenching, using the offset from the star-forming main sequence ($\Delta$SFR) as a continuous diagnostic rather than discrete quenching classes [§1, p. 2]. This matters because the link between mass distribution/dynamics and quenching is described as "relatively unexplored," and the authors want to test whether a galaxy's position relative to the main sequence (MS) is reflected in both the amplitude and the asymptotic velocity of its CVC, giving insight into how dynamics and quenching are connected [§1, p. 2].

#### Data & method
The sample is 238 CALIFA galaxies spanning morphological types E1–Sdm, the same sample as in Kalinova et al. (2017) [§2, p. 2]. CVCs come from the CALIFA CVC catalogue (Kalinova et al. 2017), derived via Jeans Anisotropic Multi-Gaussian Expansion (JAM) dynamical models assuming constant mass-to-light ratio and constant velocity anisotropy within 1.5 effective radii, built from SDSS DR7 photometry and CALIFA stellar kinematics [§2, p. 2]. Stellar masses ($M_\star$) and star-formation rates (SFR) come from Kalinova et al. (2021), derived from spatially resolved full-spectrum fitting (STARLIGHT, Chabrier IMF) of CALIFA spectra; SFR is a recent time-averaged value from summing mass formed in the last 32 Myr, chosen to be less dependent on detectable H$\alpha$ than an H$\alpha$-only estimate [§2, p. 2]. Effective radii from Kalinova et al. (2017) are used to normalize CVC radial extent [§2, p. 2]. The SFR–$M_\star$ plane is fit with the linear MS model of Elbaz et al. (2007) [§3, p. 3], and the logarithmic offset $\Delta\mathrm{SFR}\equiv\log(\mathrm{SFR})-\log(\mathrm{SFR}_{\mathrm{MS}})$ is computed per galaxy [§3, p. 3]. Galaxies are split into Blue Cloud, Green Valley ($-1.2<\Delta\mathrm{SFR}<-0.6$, following Bluck et al. 2016), and Red Sequence [§3, p. 3]. Relations between $\Delta$SFR and velocity are fit with the robust Theil–Sen estimator, with uncertainties from 1000 bootstrap iterations [§4, p. 5].

#### Main results
1. Fig. 1 [p. 3] shows the SFR–$M_\star$ diagram with the three $\Delta$SFR classes and the corresponding median CVCs and asymptotic-velocity-normalized median CVCs for each class.
2. Fig. 2 [pp. 4–5] shows individual and median CVCs per class: Blue Cloud galaxies have slow-rising prototype CVCs (40–100 km/s inner, 120–180 km/s outer); Green Valley galaxies have flat CVCs (100–270 km/s inner, 180–270 km/s outer); Red Sequence galaxies show a prominent peaked CVC (150–400 km/s inner, 270–340 km/s outer) [§4, pp. 4–5].
3. Fig. 3 and Table 1 [pp. 5–6] show that both $V_{c,\max}$ and $V_{c,\mathrm{flat}}$ increase approximately linearly with more negative $\Delta$SFR, fit with Theil–Sen slopes of $-0.154\pm0.012$ for $\log_{10}(V_{c,\max})$ and $-0.124\pm0.011$ for $\log_{10}(V_{c,\mathrm{flat}})$ [Tab. 1, p. 6]. The $V_{c,\max}$ relation is steeper by about 24% [§4, p. 5], interpreted as the inner potential (bulge/spheroid-dominated) depending more strongly on quenching than the outer potential (dark-matter-halo-dominated) [§5, p. 6].

#### Caveats & limitations
The authors note "few outliers" in the velocity–$\Delta$SFR relations, mitigated by using the outlier-resistant Theil–Sen estimator [§4, p. 5]. They frame their conclusions as consistent with "previous studies" using different categorization schemes (Kalinova et al. 2021, 2022, 2026), suggesting the result is incremental rather than a wholly new finding [§4, p. 5]. Beyond what's stated: this is a short conference proceedings paper, so there is no discussion of selection effects in the CALIFA sample, no error propagation from $M_\star$/SFR uncertainties into $\Delta$SFR, and the JAM dynamical models rest on simplifying assumptions (constant M/L and anisotropy) that are not revisited here — these are my own observations, not raised by the author. The physical interpretation (bulge vs. halo origin of $V_{c,\max}$ vs. $V_{c,\mathrm{flat}}$) is also asserted rather than directly tested with decomposed mass profiles.

#### Assessment
This is a compact, descriptive proceedings contribution rather than a methodologically novel paper — it recombines existing CALIFA dynamical and stellar-population catalogues (Kalinova et al. 2017, 2021) under a new continuous $\Delta$SFR framing instead of discrete quenching classes. The core result (steeper inner- than outer-potential response to quenching) is a reasonable, modestly interesting addition to the quenching–dynamics literature, and the use of Theil–Sen with bootstrap uncertainties is a sensible statistical choice for a modest, outlier-prone sample of 238 galaxies. For this researcher's interests, it's tangential to ML/representation learning but directly relevant to stellar-population-driven galaxy evolution and quenching studies from IFU spectroscopy (CALIFA), and could be a useful empirical anchor point for anyone combining dynamics with full-spectrum-fitting-derived SFR/$M_\star$ histories.

#### Code & data
Not mentioned (the paper references public CALIFA data and prior catalogues but gives no code or data repository links); software packages used are listed as Astropy, SciPy, NumPy, and Matplotlib [Acknowledgements, p. 6–7].

### 4. A COMPASS to the Galaxy: Joint nucleosynthetic yield set comparison and parameter inference in Galactic chemical evolution

*Berkay G\"unes, Giuseppe Viterbo, Sven Buder, Tobias Buck* · `astro-ph.GA` · `astro-ph.IM` · `cs.LG` · `physics.data-an` · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21175) · [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.21175)



**Why you might care:** Simulation-based inference (diffusion/transformer) for galactic chemical evolution yield comparison is a relevant transferable ML method though applied to Milky Way chemical evolution, not galaxy spectra. <small>(score **5.2** (you 5/10 · field 6/10))</small>

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Galactic Chemical Evolution (GCE) models connect stellar nucleosynthesis to observed abundance patterns, but their interpretation requires simultaneous parameter inference and comparison between competing stellar yield prescriptions. This remains challenging due to intractable likelihoods and growing dataset complexity. Using compass, a novel simulation-based inference framework, we perform, for the first time, amortized joint Bayesian model comparison and parameter inference for GCE models with intractable likelihoods within a unified architecture. Compass employs a conditional score-based diffusion model with a transformer architecture to learn both the posterior and likelihood directly from forward simulations, enabling efficient inference and likelihood-based ranking of competing nucleosynthetic yield sets. In controlled tests, compass identifies the generating yield set and recovers ground-truth parameters with accuracy comparable to existing simulation-based inference (SBI) methods, while reducing computational cost by orders of magnitude. Applied to Milky Way data, the framework favors a hybrid NuGrid + IllustrisTNG yield prescription, confirmed by an independent classifier, and infers an initial mass function slope of $\alpha_{\rm IMF} = -2.65 \pm 0.02$ and a SN$_{\rm Ia}$ normalization of $\log(N_{\rm Ia}) = -2.81 \pm 0.02$, with a systematic spread of $\approx 0.3$ in $\alpha_{\rm IMF}$ between the best-ranked yield sets, consistent with independent constraints. The attention mechanism confirms that the inferred parameters are driven by physically meaningful abundance signatures. By unifying amortized parameter inference, Bayesian model comparison, and interpretability in a single probabilistic framework, compass provides a scalable approach for precision chemical evolution studies with current and forthcoming spectroscopic surveys.

</details>

### 5. Orbital decomposition and evolution of LEGA-C-like galaxies in IllustrisTNG from z = 1 to z = 0
